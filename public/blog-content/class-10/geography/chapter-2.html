Biodiversity is extremely diverse on Earth and works interdependently. It is a system of closely knit networks that sustains the entire ecosystem.

India has one of the world's largest biodiversity systems — yet 10% of its recorded wild flora and 20% of its mammals are already on the threatened list.

IUCN Classification

Types of Species

Normal

Normal Species

Population levels are adequate for survival in the wild.

e.g. Pine, rodents
Endangered

Endangered Species

In danger of extinction — will decline if present conditions continue.

e.g. Crocodile, rhino, lion-tailed macaque
Vulnerable

Vulnerable Species

At risk of moving into the endangered category in the near future.

e.g. Asiatic elephant, dolphin, blue sheep
Rare

Rare Species

Small populations that could become endangered or vulnerable if conditions persist.

e.g. Asiatic buffalo, hornbill
Endemic

Endemic Species

Found only within a limited geographical area and nowhere else on Earth.

e.g. Andaman teal, Nicobar pigeon, Andaman wild pig, Mithun (Arunachal Pradesh)
Extinct

Extinct Species

No longer found in areas where they were previously known to exist.

e.g. Asiatic cheetah, pink-headed duck

⚠️ Key Causes of Flora & Fauna Depletion

  • Forest land converted to agriculture — shifting cultivation (jhum) in northeastern and central tribal belts
  • River valley projects requiring massive forest clearance since 1951
  • Large-scale mining — e.g., dolomite mining threatening Buxa Tiger Reserve, West Bengal
  • Poaching, hunting, over-exploitation, forest fires, and pollution
  • Overpopulation in developing countries placing immense pressure on natural resources
Government Action

Conservation of Forest & Wildlife

🛡️ Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972

Landmark legislation making various provisions for protecting habitats across India — the legal backbone of wildlife conservation in the country.

🦋 Wildlife Acts of 1980 & 1986

Added several hundred butterflies, moths, beetles, and one dragonfly to the list of legally protected species in India.

🐘 Partial & Full Legal Protection

Extended to Indian elephant, black buck, snow leopard, and several other species to prevent extinction.

Flagship Programme

Project Tiger — 1973

🐯 From 55,000 to 1,827 — A Crisis That Demanded Action

By 1973, India's tiger population had plunged from an estimated 55,000 to just 1,827 — driven by habitat loss, relentless poaching, depletion of prey, and illegal trade of tiger skins and bones used in traditional Asian medicines.

Launched in 1973, Project Tiger was not just about saving tigers — it was about preserving entire biotypes and the ecosystems they anchor.

Classification

Types of Forests

Type 01

Reserved Forests

Most vital for conservation. About half of total forest land is under this category — highest legal protection.

Type 02

Protected Forests

One-third of total forest area declared by the Forest Department — protected from further depletion or encroachment.

Type 03

Unclassed Forests

Owned by private individuals, government, or communities. Mostly includes forests and wasteland with varying protection.

📌 Permanent Forests

Reserved Forests + Protected Forests = Permanent Forests — maintained for collecting timber and natural products under government regulation.

People Power

Community & Conservation

⚖️

Sariska Tiger Reserve, Rajasthan

Villagers fought against illegal mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act — a landmark example of community-led legal conservation action.

🌲

Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'

Five villages in Alwar, Rajasthan declared 1,200 hectares of forest as a self-governed protected zone with strict anti-hunting rules.

🙏

Sacred Groves

Religious beliefs have preserved several virgin forests in pristine form. These 'forests of Gods and Goddesses' harbour a wealth of rare species.

🌺

Tribal Worship Traditions

Mundas and Santhals worship Mahua; Odisha & Bihar tribes revere tamarind and mango at weddings; peepal is sacred in Hindu society.

🦚

Bishnoi Villages, Rajasthan

Blackbuck, peacocks, and nilgai are considered sacred and integral to Bishnoi culture — never harmed, making villages natural sanctuaries.

🤝

Joint Forest Management (JFM)

A programme involving local communities in restoring and managing forests. First launched in Odisha in 1988 — now a national model.

Conservation Movements

People-Led Movements

🌳 Chipko Movement

Successfully saved forests by rallying communities to protect trees from being felled. Became a powerful symbol of environmental activism and inspired many follow-up conservation programmes across India.

🌾 Beej Bachao Andolan — Tehri & Navdanya

Promoted stopping synthetic chemicals by demonstrating that sufficient crop yields are achievable without chemical fertilizers — championing traditional and organic farming methods.

Practice Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What was the list of animals that were added to the protected list?

The central government announced protection for gravely threatened animals including the tiger, one-horned rhinoceros, Kashmir stag (hangul), three crocodile species — freshwater, saltwater, and Gharial — and the Asiatic lion.

Partial or full legal protection was also extended to the Indian elephant, black buck, and snow leopard. Under Wildlife Acts of 1980 and 1986, several hundred butterflies, moths, beetles, and one dragonfly were added to the protected list.

Q2. What is biodiversity?

Biodiversity refers to the enormous variety of life on Earth that works interdependently as a closely knit network sustaining the ecosystem. India hosts one of the world's largest biodiversity systems — yet 10% of its recorded wild flora and 20% of its mammals are on the threatened list.

Q3. Brief about Project Tiger.

By 1973, India's tiger population had crashed from an estimated 55,000 to just 1,827 due to habitat loss, poaching, prey depletion, and the illegal trade of tiger skins and bones. Project Tiger (1973) was launched to save tigers and preserve entire biotypes.

Key reserves: Corbett (Uttarakhand), Sunderbans (West Bengal), Bandhavgarh (MP), Sariska (Rajasthan), Manas (Assam), Periyar (Kerala).

Q4. What are permanent forests?

Reserved Forests + Protected Forests = Permanent Forests. These are maintained under strict government regulation for collecting timber and other natural products, and are legally protected from depletion or encroachment.

Q5. Why is aquatic diversity important for human communities?

Fisheries are a major income source for coastal communities. Rich aquatic diversity ensures a stable food supply and livelihood. Since all communities depend on water daily, healthy aquatic ecosystems directly support community health and food security.

Q6. What were the impacts of the Chipko Movement?

The Chipko Movement saved forests and united communities around environmental protection. Its impact includes:

  • Beej Bachao Andolan (Tehri & Navdanya) — stopped synthetic chemical use, proving organic farming yields sufficient harvests
  • Joint Forest Management (JFM) — community-led forest restoration, first started in Odisha in 1988
Q7. What were the reasons for the depletion of flora and fauna?
  • 1951–1980: Over 26,200 sq. km. of forest converted to agricultural land; shifting cultivation (jhum) in tribal belts
  • Since 1951: Over 5,000 sq. km. cleared for river valley projects (e.g., 40,000 hectares for Narmada Sagar Project)
  • Large-scale mining — dolomite mining threatening Buxa Tiger Reserve, West Bengal
  • Poaching, hunting, over-exploitation, forest fires, pollution
  • Overpopulation in developing countries as a prime cause
Q8. Explain IUCN and JFM.

IUCN — International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources. Classifies species as Normal, Endangered, Vulnerable, Rare, Endemic, or Extinct to guide global conservation priorities.

JFM — Joint Forest Management. Involves local communities in restoring and managing forests. First launched in Odisha in 1988.

Q9. What is Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'?

A community-led initiative in Alwar district, Rajasthan where five villages declared 1,200 hectares of forest as a self-governed protected zone. The community established strict anti-hunting rules and actively protects the area from outside encroachment — a model of grassroots conservation.
