💧 Geography📖 Revision Notes❓ 7 FAQs Included

Earth's surface is covered three-fourths with water — yet only a tiny fraction is usable and accessible to us. The usable portion is mainly surface run-off and groundwater, renewed by the hydrological cycle.

Despite this, water scarcity persists — driven by uneven distribution, over-exploitation, pollution, and rapid population growth.

The Problem

Causes of Water Scarcity

01

Uneven Precipitation

Seasonal and annual rainfall varies greatly by geographical region and time, leading to regional water imbalances.

02

Over-Exploitation

Rising population and unequal access leads to excessive use of water for domestic and industrial purposes — and serious pollution.

03

Irrigated Agriculture

Crops require enormous quantities of water. Individual farm pumps lower groundwater levels significantly, causing local drought and scarcity.

04

Industrial Demand

Industries consume groundwater through pumping devices and also demand hydroelectric power, putting pressure on water systems.

05

Untreated Chemical Discharge

Industries release hazardous, untreated chemicals directly into rivers, lakes, and other water bodies — rendering them unusable.

06

Unplanned Drainage

Garbage dumped into water bodies pollutes them severely, making the water unfit for any use.

Solution 1

Dams — Temples of Modern India

"Dams are the Temples of Modern India."
— Jawaharlal Nehru, on the role of dams in agriculture, village economy, industry, and urban growth

🏗️ What is a Dam?

A dam is a hydraulic structure built to store water in a reservoir by restricting the natural flow of a river. The stored water can later be released for a variety of controlled uses — from irrigation to electricity generation.

Uses of Dams

8 Major Uses of Dams

🌾

Irrigation

Supplies water for irrigating agricultural fields — the primary use.

⚡

Electricity Generation

Bhakra-Nangal Dam (Sutlej-Beas Basin) is a major source of hydel power.

🏠

Domestic Water Supply

Provides clean water for household use to towns and cities.

🏭

Industrial Supply

Supplies water needed by factories and manufacturers.

🌊

Flood Control

Hirakud Dam (Mahanadi basin) is used to control and manage seasonal floods.

🎡

Recreation

Reservoirs used for water parks and other recreational activities.

🚢

Inland Navigation

Large reservoirs support waterway transport and navigation.

🐟

Fish Breeding

Reservoirs provide controlled environments for aquaculture and fish breeding.

🏞️ Bhakra-Nangal Dam — Sutlej-Beas River Basin

One of India's largest multi-purpose dams. Widely used for hydroelectric (hydel) power production and large-scale irrigation across Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan.

🌊 Hirakud Dam — Mahanadi Basin, Odisha

Built primarily for flood control in the flood-prone Mahanadi valley. Also supports irrigation and hydroelectric power generation in the region.

The Downside

Disadvantages of Dams

  • 🪨
    Sedimentation & Fragmented Rivers: Interrupting water flow causes irregular sediment movement, increasing sedimentation in reservoirs and making aquatic migration difficult — harming biodiversity.
  • 🏘️
    Displacement of Local People: Reservoir flooding submerges villages and farmland, forcing local communities off their ancestral land.
  • 🌿
    Ecological Imbalance: High water availability encourages farmers to switch to water-intensive commercial crops, disrupting the natural ecological balance.
  • ⚖️
    Social & Inter-State Disputes: Dam water allocation creates conflicts between communities and even between states competing to prioritise their water needs.
  • 🌧️
    Floods Triggered by Dams: Sedimentation reduces reservoir capacity. During excessive rainfall, dams fail to hold water — and the controlled release of water itself can trigger devastating floods downstream.
  • 🏜️
    Land Degradation: Flood-related soil erosion (caused by dam-released water) leads to significant loss of agricultural land, property, and livelihoods.
Solution 2

Rainwater Harvesting

🌧️ What is Rainwater Harvesting?

Rainwater harvesting is the collection and storage of rainwater for future use. It has been practised in India since ancient times, adapted to local climatic conditions and geography across different regions.

Regional Methods

Harvesting Across India

🏔️
Western Himalayas

Guls & Kuls

Diversion channels — called 'guls' or 'kuls' — were built to redirect river and stream water for use in agriculture, channelling it to farms across hillside terrain.

🏜️
Rajasthan

Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting

Rainwater flows from sloped rooftops through pipes into large underground tanks (as big as a room) placed in the courtyard of homes — storing water for the dry months.

🌾
West Bengal

Inundation Channels

Special inundation channels were formed to divert monsoon floodwaters from rivers onto agricultural fields, providing controlled irrigation during the rainy season.

🌵
Arid & Semi-Arid Regions

Rain-Fed Storage Fields

Agricultural fields were designed as rain-fed storage structures — allowing rainwater to stagnate and seep slowly into the soil, keeping it moist for crops through dry periods.

Practice Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Why is water scarce despite Earth being covered with so much of it?

Earth's surface is covered three-quarters with water, but only a very small percentage is usable and accessible. Most water is saline (ocean water) or locked in glaciers. The usable portion — mainly surface run-off and groundwater — is renewed by the hydrological cycle, yet water scarcity persists due to:

  • Uneven and seasonal rainfall distribution across regions
  • Over-exploitation for domestic, industrial, and agricultural use
  • Pollution by industrial chemicals and poor drainage systems
  • Rising population creating unequal access to available resources
Q2. What are the main causes of water scarcity?
  • Uneven Precipitation: Seasonal and annual rainfall varies by geography and time, causing regional imbalances
  • Over-Exploitation: Growing population and industrial demand deplete and pollute water bodies
  • Irrigated Agriculture: Individual water pumps lower groundwater levels, causing local scarcity and drought
  • Industries: Consume groundwater, generate hydroelectric demand, and discharge untreated chemicals into water bodies
  • Unplanned Drainage: Garbage dumping into water bodies makes them unfit for use
Q3. What are dams? List their uses.

Dams are hydraulic structures built to store water in a reservoir by restricting natural water flow. They serve multiple purposes:

  • Irrigation of agricultural fields
  • Electricity generation — e.g., Bhakra-Nangal Dam on Sutlej-Beas River for hydel power
  • Domestic water supply
  • Industrial water supply
  • Flood control — e.g., Hirakud Dam on Mahanadi basin
  • Recreational purposes, inland navigation, and fish breeding
Q4. Why did Jawaharlal Nehru call dams 'Temples of Modern India'?

Nehru used this phrase because he believed dams would be transformative for the nation — powering agricultural development, village economy growth, industrial expansion, and urban development. Just as temples are central to cultural and spiritual life, he saw dams as central to India's modern economic and social progress.

Q5. What are the disadvantages of dams?
  • Interrupted sediment flow causes sedimentation in reservoirs, harming aquatic life and fragmenting river ecosystems
  • Displacement of local communities from their land
  • Encourages commercial crop farming over diverse agriculture — causes ecological imbalance
  • Creates inter-state and social disputes over water prioritisation
  • Flood-control dams can trigger floods themselves through sedimentation or uncontrolled water release during heavy rainfall
  • Land degradation — floods caused by dam releases erode soil and destroy property
Q6. What is rainwater harvesting? How is it practised across different regions of India?

Rainwater harvesting is the collection and storage of rainwater for later use. It has been practised in India since ancient times and varies by region:

  • Western Himalayas: 'Guls' or 'Kuls' — diversion channels directing water for agriculture
  • Rajasthan: Rooftop rainwater harvesting — water collected from sloped roofs into large courtyard tanks through pipes
  • West Bengal: Inundation channels formed for irrigation during monsoon flooding
  • Arid & Semi-arid regions: Agricultural fields used as rain-fed storage structures to retain water and keep soil moist
Q7. What is the significance of the Bhakra-Nangal Dam and Hirakud Dam?

Bhakra-Nangal Dam — Located on the Sutlej-Beas River Basin. One of India's largest multi-purpose dams, widely used for hydroelectric power (hydel power) production and irrigation.

Hirakud Dam — Located in the Mahanadi basin, Odisha. Built primarily for flood control in the flood-prone Mahanadi river valley, along with irrigation and power generation.
