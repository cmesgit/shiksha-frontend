Money is the lifeblood of a modern economy — it acts as a medium of exchange, eliminating the cumbersome need for a double coincidence of wants that plagued the ancient barter system.

But access to credit — and at what cost — determines who can grow and who stays trapped. Understanding the formal and informal credit systems is key to understanding India's development challenge.

1
Core Concept

Money as a Medium of Exchange

A person holding money can exchange it for any commodity or service they want. This is why everyone prefers to receive payments in money. But before money existed, people used the barter system — which had a critical flaw.

🔄
Key Concept
Double Coincidence of Wants
A situation in the barter system where what one person wants to sell is exactly what the other person wants to buy. Both parties must agree to sell and buy each other's commodities simultaneously — a condition that is very hard to meet in practice.
❌ Old System
Barter System
  • Goods exchanged directly without money
  • Requires double coincidence of wants
  • Very difficult to find the right trading partner
  • No common measure of value
  • Very inefficient for a complex economy
  • Examples: grain, cattle used as early money in India
✅ Modern System
Money Economy
  • Money acts as intermediate step in exchange
  • Eliminates need for double coincidence
  • Seller accepts money, buys what they need separately
  • Common measure of value for all goods
  • Enables complex, large-scale trade
  • Anyone can transact with anyone, anytime
2
What is Money Today

Modern Forms of Money

Before coins were introduced, a variety of objects served as money — grains and cattle in ancient India. Today, modern forms of money include currency and bank deposits, both closely linked to the banking system.

💵
Currency
  • Paper notes and coins
  • Authorised by the government of India
  • RBI issues notes on behalf of central government
  • No one else can legally issue currency
  • No one can legally refuse rupee payment
🏦
Demand Deposits
  • Money held in a bank account
  • Can be withdrawn on demand anytime
  • Banks pay interest on deposits
  • Can be used to make payments via cheque
  • Constitutes money in the modern economy
📝
Cheque
  • Paper instructing the bank to pay
  • Pays a specific amount from an account
  • Made in the name of the payee
  • Settles payments without cash
  • Works only if buyer has a bank account

"Demand deposits are accepted widely as a means of payment — along with currency, they constitute money in the modern economy. Modern forms of money are closely linked to the working of the banking system."

— NCERT Economics, Chapter 3

4
Loan Conditions

Terms of Credit

Every loan agreement specifies a set of conditions that the borrower must meet. Together, these are called the terms of credit — they determine how easy or hard it is for someone to borrow.

📈
Interest Rate
The rate the borrower must pay to the lender along with repayment of the principal amount. Formal sector interest rates are regulated; informal rates can be exploitatively high.
🏠
Collateral
An asset owned by the borrower used as a guarantee to the lender until the loan is repaid. If the borrower defaults, the lender can sell the collateral to recover the money.
📋
Documentation Requirement
Paperwork and proof required before a loan is granted — identity, income, asset ownership etc. This is a major barrier for the poor and rural borrowers.
🗓️
Mode of Repayment
The schedule and method of repayment — whether in instalments, lump sum, annually, monthly, or after harvest. Flexible repayment terms help poor borrowers significantly.
5
Two Worlds of Credit

Formal vs Informal Sector Credit

Loans in India can be grouped into two major categories based on their source. The difference between them has enormous consequences for the borrower — especially for the poor.

🏛️
Reserve Bank of India (RBI) — Regulator of Formal Credit
The RBI supervises all formal sector lenders — banks and cooperatives. Banks must maintain a minimum cash balance, periodically report their lending activities (how much, to whom, at what rate), and follow fair practices. There is no equivalent regulator for the informal sector — moneylenders operate without oversight.

Credit Inequality in India

⚠️ The Debt Trap
The higher cost of borrowing in the informal sector means a large part of the borrower's earnings goes to repaying the loan. When a poor household cannot repay, they borrow more — falling into a debt trap. This cycle prevents escape from poverty and reinforces inequality. Cheap and affordable credit is crucial for the country's development.
What Must Change
Expanding Formal Credit
  • Banks & cooperatives must increase lending in rural areas
  • Reduce dependence on informal sources of credit
  • Formal credit must be distributed more equally
  • Poor must benefit from cheaper loans
  • Address collateral barrier — explore alternatives
Why Banks Fail the Poor
Barriers to Formal Credit
  • Banks not present everywhere in rural India
  • Absence of collateral — major barrier for poor
  • Complex documentation requirements
  • Moneylenders are more accessible — known personally
  • But moneylenders charge exploitative rates
  • No record-keeping — borrowers left with no protection
7
Alternative Credit Solution

Self-Help Groups for the Poor

To bridge the gap between the poor and formal credit, a new model emerged in recent years — the Self-Help Group (SHG). It allows the poor to organise themselves, save collectively, and access credit without the barriers of the formal banking system.

Newer Model · Rural Credit
Self-Help Groups (SHGs)
A community-based solution for affordable credit access
  • Groups of poor people (mainly women) who come together to pool savings regularly
  • Members can borrow from the group pool at reasonable interest rates
  • Decisions on savings, loans, and interest rates are taken by the group democratically
  • No collateral required — social trust within the group acts as the guarantee
  • Once a group has a track record, banks are willing to lend to the entire group
  • Moneylenders cannot charge high rates — exploitation reduced significantly
  • Moneylenders also often keep no transaction records — SHGs maintain proper accounts
  • Help poor households become self-reliant and financially empowered

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is the barter system? What is double coincidence of wants?
The barter system is a system where goods are directly exchanged without the use of money. For a barter exchange to happen, both parties must want exactly what the other has to offer — this is called double coincidence of wants. For example, a farmer who wants shoes must find a cobbler who wants wheat in return. Money eliminates this problem by acting as a medium of exchange, removing the need for double coincidence.
Q2. What are modern forms of money? Who authorises currency in India?
Modern forms of money include currency (paper notes and coins) and demand deposits with banks. Currency is accepted because it is authorised by the government. In India, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) issues currency notes on behalf of the central government. No other individual or organisation is allowed to issue currency. By law, no individual in India can legally refuse a payment made in rupees.
Q3. What are demand deposits? What is a cheque?
Demand deposits are deposits with banks that can be withdrawn on demand (at any time) by the account holder. They are considered money because they can be used for payments. A cheque is a paper instructing the bank to pay a specific amount from the person's account to the person in whose name the cheque is issued. The facility of cheques against demand deposits allows payments to be settled directly without using cash.
Q4. How do banks mediate between depositors and borrowers? How do they earn income?
Banks accept deposits and pay interest to depositors. They use the major portion of these deposits to give out loans to borrowers at a higher interest rate. They keep only a small proportion as cash reserve to pay depositors who want to withdraw. Banks mediate between those with surplus funds(depositors) and those in need of funds (borrowers). Their main income is the difference between the interest charged on loans and the interest paid on deposits.
Q5. What are the terms of credit?
Every loan agreement specifies certain conditions collectively called terms of credit. They include:

• Interest rate — the rate the borrower must pay along with repayment of the principal.
• Collateral — an asset owned by the borrower used as a guarantee until the loan is repaid.
• Documentation requirement — paperwork required to apply for and obtain the loan.
• Mode of repayment — how and when the loan must be repaid.
Q6. What is collateral? Give examples.
Collateral is an asset that the borrower owns and uses as a guarantee to the lender until the loan is repaid. If the borrower fails to repay, the lender has the right to sell the collateral to recover the loan amount. Examples of collateral include: land, building, vehicle, livestock, and deposits with banks. The absence of collateral is one of the main reasons why the poor are unable to access bank loans.
Q7. What is the role of the RBI in supervising formal credit?
The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) supervises the functioning of formal sources of loans. Its role includes:

• Monitoring that banks maintain a minimum cash balance from deposits received.
• Requiring banks to periodically submit information on how much they are lending, to whom, and at what interest rate.
• Ensuring banks follow fair lending practices.

There is no such supervisory body for the informal sector — informal lenders can charge any rate and use unfair means to recover money.
Q8. What is the difference between formal and informal sector credit?
Formal sector credit comes from banks and cooperatives. It is supervised by the RBI, charges regulated interest rates, requires documentation, and is generally cheaper.

Informal sector credit comes from moneylenders, traders, employers, relatives, and friends. There is no regulation or supervision. Lenders can charge any interest rate and use unfair means to recover money. The cost of borrowing is much higher for the poor who depend on it.
Q9. Who gets formal credit and who gets informal credit in India?
There is a sharp inequality in access to credit in India:

• 85% of loans taken by poor urban households come from informal sources.
• 90% of loans taken by richer urban households come from formal sources (only 10% from informal).
• The formal sector meets only about half the total credit needs of rural people — the rest comes from informal sources.

Rich households access cheap formal credit; poor households pay high informal rates. This inequality must be addressed for equitable development.
Q10. What are Self-Help Groups (SHGs)? Why were they formed?
Self-Help Groups (SHGs) are groups of poor people (mainly women) who pool their savings and lend to members at reasonable rates. They were formed because:

• Banks are not present everywhere in rural India.
• Getting a bank loan is difficult — the absence of collateral is a major barrier.
• Moneylenders charge very high rates, keep no records, and harass poor borrowers.

SHGs provide an alternative to exploitative informal lenders and help the poor access organised, affordable credit without collateral.
Q11. Why is cheap and affordable credit important for development?
Cheap and affordable credit is crucial because:

• High cost of borrowing means a large part of earnings goes to repaying loans instead of improving living standards.
• Informal loans can trap poor households in a debt cycle — borrowing more to repay earlier debts.
• Affordable credit allows farmers, small businesses, and households to invest, grow, and improve income.
• It is necessary that formal credit is distributed more equally so that the poor benefit from cheaper loans and are not left dependent on exploitative moneylenders.
