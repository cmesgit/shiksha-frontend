Long before the word "globalisation" existed, the world was already deeply connected. Silk Routes carried silk and spices across continents. Ships carried diseases that decimated empires. Food from the Americas transformed European diets. Silver from Mexico funded Asian trade.

This chapter traces how the world became one economic system — from ancient trade routes to the Bretton Woods institutions, through famines, wars, depressions, and the remarkable resilience of ordinary people who kept the world moving.

Definition
What is Globalisation?
Globalisation is an economic system associated with the free movement of technology, goods, people, and ideas across the world. It describes the rapid and increasing interdependence of the world's cultures, populations, and economies — a change generally brought about by cross-border trade between different countries. Globalisation integrates national economies with the global economy, increasing the value of the national marketplace and helping countries gain recognition and development.

Ancient Connections

The Silk Routes

The Silk Routes were one of the most important trade and cultural arteries of the ancient world — linking distant civilisations long before the modern era. These routes existed before the Christian Era and thrived to their peak until the 15th century.

Historians have identified several Silk Routes — both land and sea links — that were used for trading crucial goods like textiles, precious metals, spices, and ideas. They were far more than trade routes: they were channels of cultural exchange.

  • A great example of booming pre-modern trade and the growing cultural relationships between distant places across the world
  • Religious preachers from Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam all travelled through these routes, spreading their faiths
  • Knowledge, goods, customs, and crafts were all exchanged — the Indian subcontinent was a key hub in this network
  • Both land routes across Central Asia and sea routes through the Indian Ocean formed part of the larger Silk Road network
  • Food items like noodles (from China) and pasta (introduced by Arabs to Sicily) spread across cultures through these very routes

The Columbian Exchange

Conquest, Trade & Disease

When European powers arrived in the Americas, the world changed forever. The Portuguese and Spanish conquest and the colonisation of America set off a chain of economic and biological consequences that reshaped every continent.

Biological Warfare
The Deadliest Weapon: Smallpox

The most deadly weapon the Spanish conquerors used was not the sword — it was smallpox germs. American original inhabitants had almost zero immunity against such diseases, and entire civilisations were wiped out by European pathogens.

Economic Impact
Silver, Minerals & Wealth
  • America's discovery opened up vast fertile lands, minerals, and new crops that paved the way for global change
  • Silver and precious metal mines in Mexico and Peru dramatically boosted Europe's wealth
  • This wealth was used to fund trade with Asian countries, fundamentally changing global commerce
  • Opium grown in India was exported to China by the British — proceeds funded the import of Chinese tea

The Engine of Globalisation

Three Flows of the Global Economy

International economic exchanges are driven by three distinct but interconnected flows. Understanding these is essential to understanding how the global economy works:

📦
Flow of Trade
Trade in goods, crops, minerals, and manufactured products across international borders — the most visible form of global connection.
👷
Flow of Labour
Migration of workers and people seeking employment — from indentured labourers shipped to the Caribbean to modern migrants crossing continents.
💰
Flow of Capital
Movement of money for both long-term investments (factories, plantations) and short-term capital flows — financing trade and development globally.
4
The Nineteenth Century

Britain, Corn Laws & Food Trade

The 19th century saw Britain transformed by industrialisation — and with it came an enormous population boom that dramatically increased demand for food grains. The political battles over food imports defined the era.

🌾 Britain's Food Economy — 19th Century
Corn Laws (1804)
Landowners who dominated Parliament imposed duties on imported corn to protect their profits — keeping bread prices artificially high
After Repeal
Cheaper imported food flooded Britain — British agriculture couldn't compete, and vast areas of land were left uncultivated
Transport Revolution
Food was transported via ships and railways — linking distant agricultural regions to British markets
Other Imports
Rubber, cotton, and coal also formed key parts of Britain's import trade alongside food
Technology
Refrigerated ships enabled animals to be butchered and shipped to Europe as frozen meat — revolutionary for global food trade

The significant technological inventions of the 19th century — the telegraph, railways, and steamships — fundamentally transformed the speed and scale of global trade. The invention of the refrigerated ship was particularly revolutionary: for the first time, meat could be butchered at the source and shipped in frozen form to distant markets in Europe.

Africa & Colonial Exploitation

Rinderpest & Africa

Africa in the late 19th century became a target of European colonial ambition — attracted by its abundant minerals and land resources. The continent's tragedy was compounded by a catastrophic disease that destroyed African self-sufficiency and forced its people into colonial labour markets.

Cattle Plague · 1880s
Rinderpest
  • Rinderpest was a deadly and rapidly spreading cattle plague that swept across Africa in the 1880s
  • It was carried by infected cattle imported from British Asia
  • The plague devastated more than half of African communities' livelihoods, which were built around cattle farming
  • Mine and plantation owners, along with colonial governments, monopolised what remained of the cattle — forcing the African population into the labour market with no alternatives
Colonial Exploitation
Why Europeans Came to Africa
  • Attracted by rich mineral resources and vast, fertile land
  • Europeans established plantations and mines, shipping extracted resources back to Europe
  • Africa's comparatively weak military could not resist the superior British and European armed forces
  • The destruction of African self-sufficiency created a captive and controlled labour force — a deliberate colonial strategy

Labour Migration from India

Indentured Labour — The New Slavery

Indentured labour referred to a bonded labourer who was under a strict contract to work for a particular employer. In practice, it was slavery by another name — a system of deception, coercion, and exploitation that stripped workers of their rights and freedom.

🇬🇾
Guyana & Caribbean Islands
  • Indian indentured labourers were widely recruited to work in Guyana, the Caribbean islands, Surinam, Fiji, and Mauritius
  • Recruitment was done by deceiving workers — false promises of good wages and conditions
  • On arrival, labourers discovered the actual harsh working and living conditions — nothing like what they had been told
  • Workers were beaten, imprisoned, and had no legal recourse against their employers
🇮🇳
India — The Source of Labour
  • Indian workers were among the most heavily recruited for indentured labour schemes across the British Empire
  • Agents falsely presented working conditions, leaving most workers unaware of the long sea journeys they would face
  • The poverty and lack of economic opportunity in India made workers vulnerable to deception
  • This form of bonded labour was finally abolished in 1921, following years of sustained protest and advocacy

1914 – 1939

The World Wars & Inter-War Economy

Over 30 nations declared war between 1914 and 1918. The majority were on the side of the Allies — Serbia, Russia, France, Britain, Italy, and the United States. They were opposed by the Central Powers — Germany, Austria-Hungary, Bulgaria, and the Ottoman Empire. Europe was the real epicentre.

The First World War didn't just destroy lives — it shattered the global economy that had taken a century to build.

— The Inter-War Economic Crisis
  • The war led to mass production of tanks, machine guns, chemical weapons, and aircraft — transforming warfare forever
  • Men of working age were recruited as soldiers from across the globe — devastating the agricultural and industrial workforce
  • Europe experienced economic hardships and political instability — which were then compounded by the Second World War just two decades later
  • Britain borrowed heavily from the USA to fund the war — ending the conflict burdened with massive external debts
  • Britain could no longer compete with Japan, the USA, and Germany in industrial production
  • Indian industries were promoted as anti-British sentiment rose — further damaging British industrial dominance

1929 – Mid 1930s

The Great Depression

The Great Depression was a period of catastrophic global economic collapse between 1929 and the mid-1930s. It was the most severe economic crisis the modern world had ever seen — and its effects reached from American boardrooms to Indian farming villages.

Root Causes
What Caused the Depression?
  • Agricultural overproduction — too much food supply drove prices down catastrophically
  • Disintegration of the US banking system — many banks went bankrupt, wiping out savings
  • Withdrawal of US loans from multiple countries, triggering global financial cascades
  • A combination of these factors created a self-reinforcing downward spiral in the global economy
Global Effects
Consequences Worldwide
  • Huge global decline in employment, production, trade, and income simultaneously
  • The agricultural sector was severely affected — prices collapsed, farms failed
  • In India, rates of exports and imports extensively declined as prices fell sharply
  • Bengal jute cultivators were among the most affected communities in India
  • A major population shift from villages to towns and cities as rural livelihoods collapsed

The Post-War New Order

Bretton Woods & Global Institutions

After the Second World War ended, the USA and USSR emerged as the two new superpowers. To prevent another devastating economic collapse, the world's nations came together to design a new global financial architecture.

🏛️ Bretton Woods Institutions
Conference
United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference — held at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, USA
World Bank
International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD) — established for post-war reconstruction and development financing
IMF
International Monetary Fund — founded to handle the external surpluses and deficits of member nations and stabilise currencies
Bretton Twins
World Bank and IMF were famously called the 'Bretton Twins' — they commenced operations in 1957
Exchange Rate
The system operated on fixed exchange rates — providing stability for international trade and investment
10
Independence & Sovereignty

Decolonisation & NIEO

In the decades following World War II, most countries in Asia and Africa gained their independence. But political freedom was not enough — newly independent nations also sought economic sovereignty and demanded a fairer share of the global economic order.

  • Most countries in Asia and Africa became independent in the post-war period
  • NAM (Non-Aligned Movement) and the UNO actively supported the independence movements
  • Developing countries coordinated themselves into the G-77 group — a coalition of 77 developing nations
  • The G-77 demanded a New International Economic Order (NIEO) — a system giving member countries complete control over their raw materials, national resources, and indigenously produced goods
  • NIEO challenged the existing global economic order dominated by wealthy industrialised nations and sought equitable access to international markets for developing countries

Globalization is not new. From silk routes to steamships, from indentured labour to the IMF — humanity has always been more connected than it realised.

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. State the effect of potatoes on the poor population of Europe and the effects of the Great Potato Famine.

Potatoes were a crop that incredibly transformed the lives of poor Europeans. They allowed the poor to feed themselves properly and live longer — a vegetable so nutritious that it single-handedly raised living standards for the working poor across the continent.

However, the Great Potato Famine of 1846–1849 was catastrophic:

  • Irish farmers became dangerously dependent on the potato as their primary crop
  • When the famine struck, approximately 1,000,000 people died of starvation between 1845 and 1851 — either from hunger or hunger-related diseases
  • A further 1 million Irish people emigrated to other countries in search of work and food
  • Ireland effectively lost a quarter of its entire population during these years — one of the most devastating demographic disasters in European history
Q2. How was indentured labour the new system of slavery?

The indentured labour system, though officially not called slavery, functioned in deeply similar ways:

  • Deception during recruitment: Poor migrant workers were recruited by agents who gave them false information about working and living conditions. Most workers were entirely unaware of the long sea journeys involved
  • Bonded contracts: Workers were under a strict contract to work for a specific employer — they had no freedom to choose their employer or leave without facing legal consequences
  • Violence and imprisonment: Workers were beaten and sometimes imprisoned for any resistance or refusal to comply
  • Terrible living conditions: On arrival, labourers discovered the actual harsh working and living conditions — very different from what they had been promised
  • This exploitative system was finally abolished in 1921
Q3. What is the Corn Law?

The Corn Law was introduced in Britain in 1804. According to this law:

  • The landowners who dominated the Parliament used their political power to protect their profits
  • A duty (tax) was imposed on imported corn, making foreign grain more expensive than domestically grown produce
  • This effectively protected British landowners from cheaper foreign competition and kept domestic bread prices artificially high
Q4. List the effects of the Corn Law.

The Corn Law had wide-ranging consequences on Britain's economy and society:

  • Growth in British wheat farming — and a sharp hike in bread prices, making basic food unaffordable for the poor
  • Widespread unemployment in the agricultural industry as the system became unsustainable
  • Boosted the rise of the capitalist class in urban areas — industrialists who wanted cheap food for their workers eventually forced the repeal of the Corn Laws
  • Mass migration from agriculture to industry — unemployed agricultural labourers shifted to the industrial division
  • After repeal, cheap food imports flooded Britain, and huge areas of agricultural land were left uncultivated, fundamentally changing the rural economy
Q5. Name the three flows within international economic exchanges.

Economists and historians identify three key flows that powered international economic exchanges:

  • Flow of Trade: The movement of goods — trade in crops, minerals, textiles, and manufactured goods across international borders
  • Flow of Labour: Migration of workers and people looking for employment — from their home countries to foreign lands in search of better opportunities
  • Flow of Capital: The movement of money and investments — both long-term investments (building factories, plantations) and short-term capital flows across the globe
Q6. Why were Europeans attracted to Africa?

Europeans were attracted to Africa for a combination of economic and strategic reasons:

  • Africa was extraordinarily rich in mineral resources and vast, fertile land that Europeans wanted to exploit
  • Europeans established multiple plantations and mines, extracting resources and importing the profits back to Europe
  • The Rinderpest cattle plague of the 1880s had already destroyed more than half of African communities' livelihoods — mine owners and colonial governments then monopolised what remained of the cattle, forcing the indigenous African population into the labour market
  • Africa had a comparatively weak military and could not effectively resist the superior British and European military forces
  • The destruction of African self-sufficiency created a captive labour force that served European colonial economic interests
Q7. How was Britain economically affected after the First World War?

The First World War left Britain economically devastated in multiple ways:

  • Britain had borrowed heavily from the United States to fund the war — leaving it burdened with massive external debts after the war ended
  • The war severely shook Britain's position in the Indian market — with the rise of anti-British sentiment in India, Indian industries were actively promoted while British industries were damaged
  • Widespread unemployment and sharp declines in both industrial and agricultural production were observed
  • Britain could no longer compete with the growing industrial powers of Japan, the USA, and Germany
  • Britain went from being the world's largest creditor nation to a debtor nation — a historic shift in the global economic order
Q8. How did food items facilitate long-distance cultural exchanges?

Food has been one of the most powerful vehicles of cultural exchange throughout history:

  • Travellers and traders introduced new crops and food items as they moved across continents and cultures
  • It is popularly believed that noodles travelled from China and were eventually transformed into Spaghetti in Italy
  • Arab traders are credited with introducing pasta to Sicily, an island in Italy — demonstrating how Arab-European trade shaped European cuisine
  • Foods like potatoes, soya, groundnuts, maize, tomatoes, and chillies from the Americas were introduced to Europe and Asia through the Columbian Exchange after 1492
  • These food exchanges were inseparable from broader cultural, religious, and commercial exchanges — trade routes were also culture routes
Q9. What is NIEO?

NIEO stands for New International Economic Order.

  • It was demanded by the G-77 group — a coalition of 77 developing countries
  • The G-77 nations sought complete control over their national resources, raw materials, and indigenously manufactured goods
  • NIEO was meant to establish a new global economic system that gave developing nations fair and equitable access to international markets
  • It was supported by NAM (Non-Aligned Movement) and the UNO as most Asian and African countries gained independence and sought economic sovereignty
  • The demand arose because the existing post-war economic order was seen as heavily biased towards the already wealthy industrialised nations
Q10. Which crop grown in India was exported by the British to China?

Opium was the crop grown in India and exported to China by the British.

  • Opium was widely cultivated across India under British colonial agricultural policy
  • The British exported opium to China on a massive scale, creating widespread addiction among the Chinese population
  • The money earned through opium exports was then used to finance the import of tea and other goods from China — funding Britain's enormous appetite for Chinese tea
  • This created the infamous triangular trade between Britain, India, and China — one of the most exploitative economic arrangements in colonial history, and a direct cause of the Opium Wars
