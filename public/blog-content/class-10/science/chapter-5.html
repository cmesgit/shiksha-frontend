With over 118 elements discovered, studying each one individually would be impossible. Periodic classification groups elements with similar properties together — making chemistry organised, predictable, and learnable.

This chapter traces the journey from Döbereiner's Triads (1817) to Newlands' Octaves, Mendeleev's genius, and finally the Modern Periodic Table — along with trends in atomic size, valency, metallic character, and ionisation energy.

1
History of Classification

Early Attempts at Classification

The classification of elements was necessary to make their study more convenient. The earliest classification was simply grouping elements as metals and non-metals. As more elements were discovered, scientists searched for deeper patterns linking atomic masses to properties.

1817DöbereinerLaw of Triads
Döbereiner was the first person to illustrate the relationship between atomic masses and properties. He arranged elements in groups of three called triads, in increasing order of atomic mass. He observed that the atomic mass of the middle element was approximately the arithmetic mean of the first and third.
The properties of the middle element were also intermediate between those of the first and third element.

2
Atomic Mass Based

Mendeleev's Periodic Table

Mendeleev arranged all 63 known elements in order of increasing atomic masses and grouped elements with similar properties in the same vertical column (group). He formulated the Mendeleev's Periodic Law:

📜
Mendeleev's Periodic Law
Properties are a Periodic Function of Atomic Mass
"The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic masses." — Mendeleev arranged elements so that elements with similar properties appeared in the same vertical group, and left gaps for undiscovered elements.
✓ Achievements
Left gaps in the periodic table for undiscovered elements — so known elements didn't need to be moved when new ones were found
Eka-Aluminium: Predicted the existence of Gallium and called it eka-aluminium — its properties matched exactly when discovered later
Eka-Silicon: Predicted Germanium (eka-silicon) with nearly perfect properties — confirmed on discovery in 1886
Noble Gases: When noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn) were discovered later, they fit into a new Group 0 without disturbing any existing element's position
✗ Limitations
Position of Hydrogen: Failed to explain the position of hydrogen — it could fit in Group 1 (like alkali metals) or Group 17 (like halogens)
Isotopes: Could not explain the position of isotopes — isotopes of the same element have different atomic masses but same chemical properties
Anomalous Pairs: Some elements with higher atomic mass were placed before elements with lower atomic mass: Ar(39.9) before K(39.1), Co before Ni, Te before I
3
Atomic Number Based

The Modern Periodic Table

⚛️
Modern Periodic Law — Henry Moseley (1913)
Properties are a Periodic Function of Atomic Number
"The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers, not their atomic masses." — This resolved all anomalies in Mendeleev's table. Atomic number (= proton number) is the true fundamental property of an element.

The modern periodic table consists of 7 Periods (horizontal rows) and 18 Groups (vertical columns). Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells; elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons.

↕️
7 Periods (Rows)
Elements in the same period have the same valence shell. Period number = number of electron shells. Period 1 has 2 elements; Period 2 and 3 have 8 each.
↔️
18 Groups (Columns)
Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons → similar chemical properties → same valency.
⬅️
Metals (Left Side)
Metals are present on the left-hand side of the periodic table. They tend to lose electrons and form positive ions.
➡️
Non-metals (Right Side)
Non-metals are present on the right-hand side. They tend to gain electrons. Metalloids lie along the dividing staircase line.

Periodic Trends

Trends in the Periodic Table

Properties of elements change in predictable patterns as we move across a period (left to right) or down a group. These are called periodic trends. Understanding them lets you predict the properties of any element from its position.

⬇ Down a Group
Same number of valence e⁻; more shells
↑Atomic Size increases — new shell added each period
↑Metallic character increases — outer e⁻ farther from nucleus, easier to lose
↓Ionisation energy decreases — outer e⁻ less tightly held
↓Electron gain enthalpy decreases
→Valency remains the SAME (same valence e⁻ count)
→ Across a Period (L to R)
Same shell; increasing nuclear charge
↓Atomic Size decreases — more protons pull electrons closer
↓Metallic character decreases — harder to lose electrons
↑Ionisation energy increases — electrons held more tightly
↑Electron gain enthalpy increases
↑↓Valency first increases (1→4) then decreases (4→0)
4a
Trend 1

Atomic Size

Atomic size refers to the radius of an atom — the distance from the nucleus to the outermost electron shell.

⬇ Down the Group
→ Across a Period (L to R)
Atomic size INCREASES ↑
Atomic size DECREASES ↓
New electron shells are added with each period
Electrons added to the SAME shell — no new shell
More shells = greater distance of outermost electrons from nucleus
Nuclear charge (protons) increases → stronger pull on electrons → smaller radius
Inner shells also shield outer electrons from nuclear pull
All electrons in the same shell → shielding is minimal
Example: Li < Na < K < Rb < Cs (down Group 1)
Example: Na > Mg > Al > Si > P > S > Cl (Period 3)
4b
Trend 2

Valency Trends

Valency is the combining capacity of an element, determined by the number of electrons in the outermost shell (valence electrons). The valence shell must have 8 electrons (octet) for stability — valency is either the number of valence electrons (if ≤ 4) or 8 minus that number (if > 4).

1️⃣
Group 1 — Valency 1
Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs — 1 valence e⁻ → lose 1 to form M⁺
2️⃣
Group 2 — Valency 2
Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba — 2 valence e⁻ → lose 2 to form M²⁺
3️⃣
Group 13 — Valency 3
B, Al, Ga, In — 3 valence e⁻ → lose 3 or form 3 bonds
4️⃣
Group 14 — Valency 4
C, Si, Ge, Sn — 4 valence e⁻ → form 4 covalent bonds
3️⃣
Group 15 — Valency 3
N, P, As — 5 valence e⁻ → needs 3 more (8−5=3)
2️⃣
Group 16 — Valency 2
O, S, Se — 6 valence e⁻ → needs 2 more (8−6=2)
1️⃣
Group 17 — Valency 1
F, Cl, Br, I — 7 valence e⁻ → needs 1 more (8−7=1)
0️⃣
Group 18 — Valency 0
He, Ne, Ar — 8 valence e⁻ (full octet) → inert/noble
✅ Key rule: Valency remains same down the group (same number of valence electrons). Across a period, valency first increases (1→4) then decreases (3→2→1→0).
4c
Trend 3

Metallic Character

Metallic character is the tendency of an atom to lose electrons and form positive ions. Higher metallic character = lower ionisation energy = larger atomic size.

⬇ Down the Group
→ Across Period (L to R)
Metallic character INCREASES ↑
Metallic character DECREASES ↓
Atomic size increases → outer electrons farther away
Atomic size decreases → outer electrons held tightly
Outer electrons more easily lost → easier to form M⁺
Harder to lose electrons → non-metallic character increases
Example: Na is less metallic than K, which is less than Rb
Example: Na > Mg > Al > Si > P > S > Cl > Ar

"Metals are on the left side of the periodic table, non-metals on the right — because metallic character decreases from left to right across a period. The most reactive metal (Caesium/Francium) is at the bottom-left; the most reactive non-metal (Fluorine) is at the top-right."

4d
Trend 4

Ionisation Energy & Electron Gain Enthalpy

Ionisation energy is the energy required to remove the outermost electron from a neutral gaseous atom. Electron gain enthalpy is the energy change when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom. Both follow the same periodic trend.

📊 Ionisation Energy & Electron Gain Enthalpy — Trends
Both ionisation energy and electron gain enthalpy follow the same pattern: they increase left to right across a period and decrease down a group.
↑ Increases — Across Period (L→R)
Nuclear charge increases. Atomic size decreases. Outermost electrons held more tightly. More energy needed to remove them. Non-metallic character increases.
↓ Decreases — Down a Group
Atomic size increases. More electron shells provide greater shielding. Outer electrons are less tightly held. Less energy needed to remove them. Metallic character increases.
⚡ Connection: High ionisation energy = hard to lose electrons = non-metallic character. Low ionisation energy = easy to lose electrons = metallic character. Fluorine has the highest electron gain enthalpy — it most readily accepts electrons.
5
Why the Table Works

Periodicity in Properties

Elements show periodicity because of their valence shell electronic configuration. As we move through the periodic table in order of increasing atomic number, after one shell is completely filled, the next shell starts filling — and elements with the same number of valence electrons repeat similar properties at regular intervals.

All elements showing periodicity in properties have the same number of electrons in the last (valence) shell. For example:

Element
Configuration → Period & Group
Li (Z=3)
2,1 → Period 2, Group 1 — 1 valence e⁻
Na (Z=11)
2,8,1 → Period 3, Group 1 — 1 valence e⁻ (same as Li)
K (Z=19)
2,8,8,1 → Period 4, Group 1 — 1 valence e⁻ (same as Na)
O (Z=8)
2,6 → Period 2, Group 16 — 6 valence e⁻
S (Z=16)
2,8,6 → Period 3, Group 16 — 6 valence e⁻ (same as O)
Ne (Z=10)
2,8 → Period 2, Group 18 — 8 valence e⁻ (complete octet)
Ar (Z=18)
2,8,8 → Period 3, Group 18 — 8 valence e⁻ (same as Ne)
✅ Summary: Same group = same valence electrons = same chemical properties. Period number = number of electron shells. Atomic number increases left to right and top to bottom. This is why the periodic table is the most powerful tool in chemistry.
Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Why was the classification of elements necessary?
As more and more elements were discovered, studying them individually became very difficult and confusing. Classification was necessary to organise elements into groups based on similar properties, making their study more convenient and systematic.

The earliest classification simply divided elements into metals and non-metals. Over time, more sophisticated classification systems were developed — first by Döbereiner, then Newlands, then Mendeleev, and finally the modern periodic table.
Q2. What is Döbereiner's Law of Triads? Give examples.
Döbereiner was the first to show a relationship between atomic masses and properties of elements. He observed that when elements were arranged in groups of three (triads) in increasing order of atomic mass, the atomic mass of the middle element was approximately the average of the first and third.

Examples of Döbereiner's Triads:
• Li (7), Na (23), K (39) — Na ≈ (7+39)/2 = 23 ✓
• Ca (40), Sr (88), Ba (137) — Sr ≈ (40+137)/2 = 88.5 ✓
• Cl (35.5), Br (80), I (127) — Br ≈ (35.5+127)/2 = 81.3 ✓

Limitation: Not all known elements could be grouped into triads — only a few worked.
Q3. What is Newlands' Law of Octaves? What are its limitations?
Newlands arranged all known elements in increasing order of atomic mass and observed that every 8th element had properties similar to the 1st element — like notes in a musical octave (sa re ga ma pa dha ni).

For example: H, Li, Be, B, C, N, O — the 8th element F had properties similar to H.

Limitations of Newlands' Law of Octaves:
• It worked only for lighter elements up to calcium — failed for heavier elements
• When noble gases were discovered later, the law completely broke down
• He placed two elements in the same slot (like Co and Ni) to maintain the pattern
• Some unlike elements were placed together — e.g., Fe was placed far from Co and Ni, which are chemically similar
Q4. What is Mendeleev's Periodic Law? What are his achievements?
Mendeleev's Periodic Law: "The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic masses."

He arranged all 63 known elements in order of increasing atomic mass into rows (periods) and columns (groups) based on similar properties.

Achievements of Mendeleev's Periodic Table:
1. He left gaps for undiscovered elements so that known elements didn't have to be displaced — and predicted their properties accurately
2. He predicted the existence of Gallium and called it eka-aluminium (later discovered and matched his predictions exactly)
3. He predicted Germanium (eka-silicon) and Scandium (eka-boron)
4. When noble gases were discovered later (He, Ne, Ar…), they were placed in a new Group 0 without disturbing any other element's position
Q5. What are the limitations of Mendeleev's Periodic Table?
Limitations of Mendeleev's Periodic Table:

1. Position of hydrogen was unexplained — H has properties of both alkali metals (Group 1) and halogens (Group 17). Mendeleev could not decide where to place it.

2. Position of isotopes was not explained — Isotopes of the same element have different atomic masses. If arranged by atomic mass, they would need separate positions, but they have the same chemical properties.

3. Anomalous pairs — Some elements with higher atomic mass were placed before elements with lower atomic mass to maintain chemical similarity:
• Argon (39.9) placed before Potassium (39.1)
• Cobalt (58.9) placed before Nickel (58.6)
• Tellurium (127.6) placed before Iodine (126.9)
Q6. What is the Modern Periodic Law? How is it different from Mendeleev's?
Modern Periodic Law (Henry Moseley, 1913): "The properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers."

Moseley discovered that atomic number (number of protons), not atomic mass, is the more fundamental property of an element.

Key difference from Mendeleev:
• Mendeleev used atomic mass as the basis — led to anomalous pairs
• Modern law uses atomic number — all anomalies are resolved
• The position of isotopes is now justified — they have the same atomic number, so they go in the same position
• The position of hydrogen remains debatable but is placed in Group 1 by convention
Q7. Describe the structure of the Modern Periodic Table.
The Modern Periodic Table has:

• 7 Periods (horizontal rows) — elements in the same period have the same number of occupied electron shells (same valence shell)
• 18 Groups (vertical columns) — elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons and similar chemical properties

Classification of elements:
• Groups 1, 2 and 13–18: Representative elements (s-block and p-block)
• Groups 3–12: Transition elements (d-block)
• Period 6, atomic nos. 58–71: Lanthanoids (f-block)
• Period 7, atomic nos. 90–103: Actinoids (f-block)

Position of metals vs non-metals:
• Metals — left-hand side of the table
• Non-metals — right-hand side of the table
• Metalloids (semiconductors like Si, Ge) — along the staircase dividing line
Q8. How does atomic size vary in the periodic table?
Atomic size = radius of an atom (distance from nucleus to outermost electron shell).

Down the group (increasing atomic number in same group):
→ Atomic size INCREASES
Reason: A new electron shell is added with each period, increasing the distance of outermost electrons from the nucleus. Also, inner shell electrons shield the outer electrons from nuclear attraction.

Across a period (left to right):
→ Atomic size DECREASES
Reason: The number of protons (nuclear charge) increases while electrons are added to the same shell. Greater nuclear pull draws electrons closer to the nucleus, reducing atomic size.
Q9. How does valency change across a period and down a group?
Valency is the combining capacity of an element — determined by the number of valence electrons (or electrons needed to complete the octet).

Down a group:
→ Valency remains the SAME
Reason: All elements in a group have the same number of valence electrons. E.g., all alkali metals (Group 1) have valency 1.

Across a period (left to right):
→ Valency first increases then decreases
Pattern: 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 1 → 0
Example for Period 3: Na(1) Mg(2) Al(3) Si(4) P(3) S(2) Cl(1) Ar(0)

This happens because valency depends on the number of electrons in the outermost shell, which increases from 1 to 4 and then the tendency to gain electrons kicks in (8−n pattern).
Q10. How does metallic character change in the periodic table?
Metallic character refers to the tendency of an atom to lose electrons (form positive ions). It is related to ionisation energy — lower ionisation energy = higher metallic character.

Down a group:
→ Metallic character INCREASES
Reason: Atomic size increases → outermost electrons are farther from nucleus → held less tightly → easier to lose → more metallic

Across a period (left to right):
→ Metallic character DECREASES
Reason: Nuclear charge increases → electrons held more tightly → harder to lose → less metallic → more non-metallic

This is why metals are on the left side of the periodic table and non-metals are on the right side.
Q11. What is ionisation energy and how does it vary in the periodic table?
Ionisation energy is the energy required to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom.

Across a period (left to right):
→ Ionisation energy INCREASES
Reason: Nuclear charge increases, atomic size decreases, outermost electrons are held more tightly → more energy needed to remove them

Down a group:
→ Ionisation energy DECREASES
Reason: Atomic size increases, more electron shells provide shielding → outer electrons are less tightly held → less energy needed to remove them

Electron gain enthalpy (energy released when an electron is added) follows the same trend — increases across a period (left to right) and decreases down a group.
Q12. Why do elements in the same group show similar properties?
Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their valence (outermost) shell.

Chemical properties of elements are determined primarily by their valence electrons — they determine:
• How many bonds the element forms (valency)
• Whether it tends to lose or gain electrons
• What compounds it forms

Since all elements in a group have the same number of valence electrons, they exhibit similar chemical behaviour. For example:
• All Group 1 elements (Li, Na, K…) have 1 valence electron → all react vigorously with water → all form MOH
• All Group 17 elements (F, Cl, Br, I) have 7 valence electrons → all need 1 electron → all form HX acids
Q13. What is meant by periodicity in properties? Why do elements show periodicity?
Periodicity means the regular repetition of properties of elements at regular intervals when arranged in order of increasing atomic number.

Elements show periodicity because of their valence shell electronic configuration. When we move through the periodic table, after filling one shell completely, a new shell starts filling — and elements with the same number of valence electrons repeat similar chemical properties.

For example:
• Period 2 starts with Li (2,1) and ends with Ne (2,8)
• Period 3 starts with Na (2,8,1) — similar to Li — and ends with Ar (2,8,8)

Na and Li both have 1 valence electron → same group → same periodicity in properties. This pattern repeats across every period.
