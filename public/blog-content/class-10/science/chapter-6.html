Chapter Overview

The Four Essential Life Processes

A living organism is defined not just by its structure, but by the processes it performs to stay alive. These life processes include obtaining and using food (nutrition), breaking food down for energy (respiration), moving substances within the body (transportation), and removing metabolic wastes (excretion).

This chapter studies each of these processes in both plants and humans — from the elegant chemistry of a leaf in sunlight to the beating of a four-chambered heart.

Moving Substances Around

Transportation in Plants & Humans

Transportation is a life process where substances synthesised or absorbed in one part of the organism are carried to other parts. In plants, xylem and phloem serve this role; in humans, the circulatory system does.

Water Transport
🌊 Xylem
Carries: Water + Minerals (upward)
  • Source: Soil → Roots → Stem → Leaves
  • Transpiration pull — main force driving water upward
  • Root pressure
  • Difference in pressure gradient
  • One-way upward transport
  • Dead cells form xylem vessels
Food Transport
🍃 Phloem
Carries: Food / Sucrose (both directions)
  • Source: Leaves → all other parts
  • Process called translocation
  • Requires energy (ATP) from respiration
  • Components: sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma, fibres
  • Transports sucrose, amino acids, hormones
  • Living cells form phloem
❤️
Human Circulatory System
Heart, Blood & Blood Vessels
Double Circulation
Blood passes through the heart twice per cardiac cycle — pulmonary circuit (heart→lungs→heart) and systemic circuit (heart→body→heart).
Blood Components
RBCs (carry O₂ via haemoglobin), WBCs (immunity), Platelets (clotting), Plasma (transports nutrients, hormones, CO₂).
Blood Vessels
Arteries — oxygenated blood (except pulmonary artery). Veins — deoxygenated blood (except pulmonary vein). Capillaries — exchange of gases and nutrients.
Lymphatic System
Transports immune cells (lymphocytes). Lymph — a colourless fluid — returns to the blood after collecting waste from tissues.

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is photosynthesis? Write the equation.
Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants synthesise food from CO₂ and water in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll.

Equation:
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (in presence of Sunlight and Chlorophyll)
Or simply: Carbon dioxide + Water → Carbohydrate + Oxygen

Raw materials: Chlorophyll, sunlight, CO₂, water
Products: Glucose, oxygen (and water)
Site: Leaves (contain chlorophyll in chloroplasts)
Q2. What are the two phases of photosynthesis?
1. Photochemical Phase (Light reaction):
Requires light. Occurs in thylakoid membranes. Involves:
• NADP⁺ + e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH (enzyme-catalysed)
• 2O → O₂ (Photolysis of water)
• ADP + Phosphate → ATP (Phosphorylation)

2. Biosynthetic Phase (Dark reaction / Calvin cycle):
Does NOT require light directly — but happens during daytime as well. CO₂ is fixed using ATP and NADPH produced in the light reaction to form glucose. This is where carbohydrates are actually manufactured.

Evidence: Presence of starch in leaves confirms photosynthesis has occurred.
Q3. How is sunlight and chlorophyll proved essential for photosynthesis?
Experiment 1 — Sunlight is essential:
1. Place a green plant in dark room for 1–2 days (to use up all stored starch).
2. Cover part of a leaf with black paper clips on both sides.
3. Expose plant to bright sunlight for a few hours.
4. Remove the leaf, decolourise with alcohol, test with iodine solution.
Result: Covered (dark) portion shows NO starch; uncovered portion shows blue-black colour (starch present). ✓ Sunlight is essential.

Experiment 2 — Chlorophyll is essential:
1. Use a variegated plant (has green and non-green patches e.g. croton).
2. Keep in dark 2–3 days, then expose to sunlight for 6 hours.
3. Pluck a leaf, trace the green areas, decolourise, test with iodine.
Result: Only the green (chlorophyll-containing) areas turn blue-black. ✓ Chlorophyll is essential.
Q4. Describe the human digestive system — all organs and their functions.
Alimentary canal: Mouth → Pharynx → Oesophagus → Stomach → Small Intestine → Large Intestine → Rectum → Anus

Mouth: Teeth (molars/premolars — chewing; canines — tearing; incisors — cutting). Salivary amylase breaks starch → sugar. Tongue helps chewing and swallowing.

Oesophagus: Carries food to stomach via peristalsis (wave-like muscle movement).

Stomach: Secretes mucus (protects lining), HCl (acidic medium, kills bacteria), digestive juices (pepsin — proteins → polypeptides; rennin — milk protein → curd).

Small intestine (7.5 m long): Complete digestion of carbohydrates, proteins, fats. Villi increase absorption surface. Liver (bile — fat emulsification) and pancreas (amylase — starch; lipase — fats) assist here. Maltase, sucrase, lactase, peptidase complete digestion.

Large intestine: Absorbs water and salts from undigested material.
Rectum/Anus: Stores and egests faeces (excretion of undigested waste).
Q5. How does Amoeba feed? What is holozoic nutrition?
Holozoic nutrition involves ingestion of solid food, its digestion, absorption, and egestion of undigested waste.

Amoeba's feeding process:
1. Amoeba senses prey (algae, protozoans, rotifers, other small Amoeba).
2. It extends pseudopodia (false feet — finger-like projections) around the prey.
3. The prey gets engulfed and trapped in a food vacuole.
4. Digestive enzymes amylase (breaks complex carbohydrates) and protease (breaks proteins) are secreted into the food vacuole.
5. Digested food is absorbed for growth, maintenance, and multiplication.
6. Undigested food is egested out via pseudopodia.
Q6. Compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration.
Aerobic respiration:
• Uses molecular oxygen to oxidise food
• Yields 38 ATP molecules
• Step 1: Glucose (6C) → Pyruvate (3C) in cytoplasm (glycolysis)
• Step 2: Pyruvate → CO₂ + H₂O in mitochondria (ATP produced)
• Complete oxidation of glucose

Anaerobic respiration:
• Does NOT use oxygen
• Yields only 2 ATP molecules
• Step 1: Glycolysis in cytoplasm → 2 pyruvate
• Step 2: In yeast → ethanol + CO₂ + energy; In muscle cells → lactic acid + energy
• Lactic acid build-up in muscles causes cramps during heavy exercise
Q7. How does transportation occur in plants? Explain xylem and phloem.
Transportation in plants moves food, water, and minerals between different parts.

Xylem: Conducts water and minerals from soil (via roots) upward to leaves and other parts.
Transport occurs due to: (1) Transpiration pull — water evaporating from stomata creates a suction force; (2) Root pressure; (3) Difference in pressure gradient.

Phloem: Transports food (mainly glucose/sucrose) from leaves (where it is made) to all other parts of the plant — including roots, growing shoots, fruits etc.
Components: companion cells, sieve tubes, phloem parenchyma, fibres.
Translocation through phloem requires energy in the form of ATP (obtained from respiration).

Key difference: Xylem = upward water transport; Phloem = food transport in both directions.
Q8. Describe the human circulatory system. What is double circulation?
Circulatory system consists of: the heart, blood vessels (arteries, veins, capillaries), and blood.

Blood components: RBCs (carry oxygen via haemoglobin), WBCs (immunity), platelets (clotting), plasma (transports dissolved nutrients, CO₂, hormones).

Blood vessels:
• Arteries — carry oxygenated blood (except pulmonary artery)
• Veins — carry deoxygenated blood (except pulmonary vein)
• Capillaries — thin-walled, allow exchange of gases/nutrients

Heart chambers: Right auricle, right ventricle (receives/pumps deoxygenated blood from body), left auricle, left ventricle (receives/pumps oxygenated blood from lungs).

Double circulation: Blood passes through the heart twice in each cardiac cycle — once through the pulmonary circuit (heart → lungs → heart) and once through the systemic circuit (heart → body → heart). Ensures efficient delivery of oxygen.
Q9. Describe the human excretory system. How is urine formed?
Excretion is the removal of harmful metabolic waste products from the body.

Human excretory system: Pair of kidneys → ureters → urinary bladder → urethra

Kidney: Main excretory organ. Divided into outer cortex and inner medulla. Basic unit = nephron (components: glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, renal tubule).

Urine formation in nephron:
1. Blood is filtered in glomerulus (under pressure) into Bowman's capsule
2. Useful substances (glucose, water, salts) are reabsorbed in renal tubule
3. Remaining waste fluid (urine) passes to ureter

Urine composition: 95% water, 2.5% urea, 2.5% other waste products. Human kidneys produce 1–1.8 L urine per day.

Dialysis: Used when kidneys fail — blood is filtered through an artificial kidney machine.
Q10. How do plants excrete waste products?
Plants use several strategies to remove metabolic wastes:

1. Stomata: Oxygen (by-product of photosynthesis) and CO₂ (from respiration) are released through stomata.
2. Transpiration: Excess water absorbed from roots is lost as water vapour through stomata. This is the main way plants lose water.
3. Vacuoles: Some waste products (resins, gums, tannins) are stored in cell vacuoles.
4. Old xylem tissues: Waste materials are stored here permanently.
5. Leaves: Some waste products accumulate in leaves — when leaves fall, wastes are removed with them.
6. Roots: Some plants excrete waste materials through their roots into the soil.
Q11. What is transpiration? What is its significance?
Transpiration is the evaporation of water from plant surfaces, primarily through stomata on leaves.

Process: Water absorbed by roots travels up through xylem vessels to leaves. At the leaf surface, water evaporates through stomata openings into the atmosphere.

Significance / Importance:
1. Creates transpiration pull — the main force that drives upward movement of water in tall plants
2. Cools the plant (evaporative cooling)
3. Helps in absorption and upward movement of mineral nutrients
4. Maintains water balance in the plant
5. Acts as a method of excretion — removes excess water

Stomata: Each stoma is surrounded by two guard cells that control opening and closing.
Q12. What is the role of haemoglobin in transport?
Haemoglobin is the red-coloured respiratory pigment found in RBCs (red blood cells).

Functions:
1. Oxygen transport: Haemoglobin combines with oxygen in the lungs (where O₂ concentration is high) to form oxyhaemoglobin. This is then transported to body cells where O₂ is released for cellular respiration.
2. CO₂ transport: Some CO₂ (waste product of respiration) is transported by haemoglobin back to the lungs for exhalation. (Most CO₂ is transported as bicarbonate ions in plasma.)

Without haemoglobin: Blood would carry far too little oxygen to sustain life — haemoglobin increases blood's oxygen-carrying capacity about 70 times.
