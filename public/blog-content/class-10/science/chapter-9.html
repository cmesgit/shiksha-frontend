01Heredity & Traits
1
Inheritance

Acquired vs Inherited Traits

Heredity is the transmission of characteristics from parents to offspring. Variations are differences among individuals of a species. Basis of heredity: each trait is influenced by both maternal and paternal DNA.

Not Heritable
Acquired Traits
Traits developed during an individual's lifetime due to environment or experience. NOT passed to offspring.
Changes in non-reproductive (somatic) tissue only
Cannot be passed on to progeny
Examples: scar from injury, developed muscles, tanned skin
Not encoded in DNA of gametes
Lost when the individual dies
Heritable
Inherited Traits
Traits encoded in DNA and transmitted from parents to offspring through gametes (reproductive cells).
Changes in DNA or reproductive tissue
Transmitted to progeny through gametes
Examples: eye colour, blood group, height, ear lobe shape
Basis of heredity — present in every cell of offspring
Can show variation due to sexual reproduction mixing DNA
Dominant Trait
e.g., T = Tallness
Trait that expresses itself over another contrasting trait. Written in upper case.
Recessive Trait
e.g., t = shortness
Trait that cannot express in presence of dominant. Written in lower case.
Homozygous
e.g., TT or tt
Both factors/alleles for a trait are the same (pure breeding).
Heterozygous
e.g., Tt
Two different alleles for a trait are present (hybrid).
Genotype
e.g., TT, Tt, tt
Genetic constitution of an organism — the actual allele combination.
Phenotype
e.g., Tall, dwarf
Observable physical traits or characteristics of an organism.
Genotypic Ratio
1:2:1 in F2
Expected ratio of genotypes produced by a particular cross.
Phenotypic Ratio
3:1 in monohybrid
Expected ratio of phenotypes produced by a particular cross.
Alleles
T and t
Alternative forms of the same gene occupying the same locus on homologous chromosomes.
02Mendel's Experiments
2
Garden Pea Experiments

Mendel's Work & Laws

Gregor Mendel performed experiments on Pisum sativum (garden pea) using 7 contrasting pairs of characters. He proposed that heredity is controlled by discrete units called factors — now known as genes.

🧪
Stages of Mendel's Monohybrid Experiment
Tall (TT) × Dwarf (tt)
🌱
Selection of Parents
Selected true-breeding plants with contrasting traits. Pure tall (TT) and pure dwarf (tt) pea plants. True-breeding = same phenotype for many generations.
🤝
Cross-Pollination → F1 Generation
Crossed TT × tt. All F1 offspring were heterozygous tall (Tt). Only the dominant trait (tallness) was visible. Recessive trait (dwarfism) was hidden but not lost.
🔄
Self-Pollination of F1 → F2 Generation
F1 plants (Tt) were self-pollinated. F2 generation gave ratio: 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf. Phenotypic ratio = 3:1. Genotypic ratio = 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt = 1:2:1.
🌾
Dihybrid Cross
Crossed Round Yellow (RRYY) × Wrinkled Green (rryy). F1: all RrYy (Round Yellow). F2 phenotypic ratio: 9 Round Yellow : 3 Round Green : 3 Wrinkled Yellow : 1 Wrinkled Green = 9:3:3:1.

First Law
Law of Dominance
Only one parental trait (dominant) gets expressed in F1 generation
Both dominant and recessive traits get expressed in F2 generation
Recessive trait is not lost — just masked by dominant in F1
Based on observations of monohybrid cross
Second Law
Law of Segregation
Two alleles for a trait segregate from each other during gamete formation
Each gamete receives only one allele of each gene pair
Alleles reunite at fertilisation
Also called Law of Purity of Gametes
Third Law
Law of Independent Assortment
Members of different pairs of alleles assort independently into gametes
One trait's inheritance does not affect another's
Based on dihybrid cross observations
Gives 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio in dihybrid F2
03Types of Dominance
3
Beyond Simple Dominance

Complete, Incomplete & Co-dominance

Type 1
Complete Dominance
One allele completely masks the other in heterozygous condition
Phenotypic ratio in F2: 3:1
Genotypic ratio in F2: 1:2:1
Example: Tall (T) dominant over Dwarf (t) in pea plant
Type 2
Incomplete Dominance
Neither allele completely dominant — intermediate phenotype in Rr
Both phenotypic and genotypic ratios = 1:2:1
Example: Snapdragon — Red (RR) × White (rr) → Pink (Rr)
Pink flowers in F1; 1 Red : 2 Pink : 1 White in F2
Type 3
Co-dominance
Both alleles equally dominant — both expressed together in heterozygote
Example: ABO blood group in humans
Blood group AB — both Iᴬ and Iᴮ expressed simultaneously
Multiple alleles — ABO controlled by 3 alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i

05Evolution
5
How Species Change Over Time

Causes of Evolution

Evolution is the change in heritable characteristics of biological populations over successive generations. It leads to new species through accumulated changes. Speciation = formation of new species.

🌿
Natural Selection
Darwin's theory — survival of the fittest
Individuals better adapted to environment survive and reproduce more
Favourable traits increase in population frequency over generations
Unfavourable traits decrease and may be eliminated
Example: Antibiotic resistance in bacteria, dark moths on soot-covered trees
🎲
Genetic Drift
Accidental/random change in allele frequency in small populations
NOT driven by adaptation — purely by chance
Can cause beneficial traits to disappear, harmful traits to spread
Bottleneck effect — small population after disaster may not represent original gene pool
🏔️
Geographical Isolation
Physical barrier separates a population into two groups
Separated groups cannot interbreed
Each group accumulates different mutations and adaptations
Over many generations → become too different to interbreed = new species
Speciation — the formation of new distinct species

Fossils · Organs · Classification

Homologous, Analogous & Fossils

Divergent Evolution
Homologous Organs
Similar in structure and origin but perform different functions. Evidence of common ancestry.
Same basic bone structure — humerus, radius, ulna, carpals
Different functions in different organisms
Human arm (grasping) · Whale flipper (swimming)
Bat wing (flying) · Horse forelimb (running)
Shows divergent evolution from a common ancestor
Also called: Vestigial organs (e.g., human appendix, coccyx)
Convergent Evolution
Analogous Organs
Similar in function but different in structure and origin. Different organisms evolved similar solutions.
Different structure and origin — not from same ancestor
Same function due to similar environmental pressures
Bird wing vs Insect wing — both for flying, totally different structure
Eye of octopus vs Eye of vertebrate — similar function, different origin
Shows convergent evolution — different lineages, similar adaptations

Human Origin
We All Come From Africa
All modern humans are Homo sapiens — a single species. About 200,000 years ago, our ancestors originated in Africa. Some migrated out; others stayed. The migrants spread across the entire planet. There is no biological basis for human "races" — all humans are one species. We differ only in surface features shaped by local environments over thousands of years. Humans are just one branch of evolution — not the "highest" or culminating species.

"Evolution simply creates more complex body designs — but bacteria with the simplest body design are still the most widely found organisms on Earth. Progress is not the right word for evolution. Adaptation is."

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is heredity? What is the difference between acquired and inherited traits?
Heredity is the transmission of characteristics or traits from parents to offspring through DNA.

Acquired traits:
• Traits an individual develops during their lifetime (e.g., a scar from injury, a developed muscle from exercise)
• Involves changes in non-reproductive (somatic) tissues
• Cannot be passed on to progeny — not encoded in DNA

Inherited traits:
• Traits received from ancestors; encoded in DNA
• Involves changes in DNA or reproductive tissues
• Transmitted to offspring through gametes
• Examples: eye colour, blood group, height

Variations are differences among individuals of a species. They can be heritable (genetic, passed to offspring) or non-heritable (acquired during lifetime).
Q2. Who was Gregor Mendel? What did he discover?
Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) was an Austrian monk who is called the Father of Genetics. He conducted experiments on Pisum sativum (garden pea) and proposed that heredity is controlled by discrete units called factors (now called genes).

Why pea plants?
• Easy to grow and have short generation time
• Have clearly contrasting traits (tall/dwarf, round/wrinkled seeds, etc.)
• Can be self-pollinated or cross-pollinated easily
• Used 7 pairs of contrasting characters

Key terms introduced by Mendel:
• Dominant — trait that expresses itself (e.g., T = Tall)
• Recessive — trait masked by dominant (e.g., t = dwarf)
• Homozygous — both factors same (TT or tt)
• Heterozygous — factors different (Tt)
Q3. Explain the monohybrid cross. What ratios are obtained?
Monohybrid cross involves only one pair of contrasting characters.

Example — Tall (TT) × Dwarf (tt):

F1 generation: All plants are Tt (heterozygous tall). Only tall phenotype visible — dominant trait expressed.

F2 generation (self-pollination of F1 Tt × Tt):
• TT (pure tall) — 1
• Tt (hybrid tall) — 2
• tt (pure dwarf) — 1

Phenotypic ratio: 3 Tall : 1 Dwarf = 3:1
Genotypic ratio: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt = 1:2:1

Laws derived from monohybrid cross:
1. Law of Dominance — only one trait expressed in F1; both in F2
2. Law of Segregation — two alleles separate during gamete formation
Q4. What is a dihybrid cross? What is the Law of Independent Assortment?
Dihybrid cross involves two pairs of contrasting characters.

Example: Round Yellow seeds (RRYY) × Wrinkled Green seeds (rryy)

F1: All RrYy — Round Yellow (both dominant traits expressed)

F2 phenotypic ratio: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1
• 9 Round Yellow
• 3 Round Green
• 3 Wrinkled Yellow
• 1 Wrinkled Green

Law of Independent Assortment: Members of different pairs of alleles assort independently into gametes. This means the inheritance of one trait does not affect the inheritance of another (for genes on different chromosomes).
Q5. What is incomplete dominance? What is co-dominance?
Incomplete Dominance:
One allele is not completely dominant over the other. The heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype.
Example: Snapdragon flower — Red (RR) × White (rr) → Pink (Rr) in F1
F2: 1 Red : 2 Pink : 1 White
Both phenotypic and genotypic ratios are 1:2:1

Co-dominance:
Both alleles are equally dominant and expressed simultaneously in heterozygous condition.
Example: ABO blood group in humans
• Blood group AB — both Iᴬ and Iᴮ alleles expressed together
• ABO blood group is also an example of multiple alleles (3 alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i control one trait)

Key difference: Incomplete dominance → blended intermediate; Co-dominance → both expressed distinctly
Q6. How is sex determined in humans?
Chromosomal basis of sex determination:

Humans have 46 chromosomes = 22 pairs of autosomes + 1 pair of sex chromosomes

• Females: 44 + XX — two X chromosomes
• Males: 44 + XY — one X and one Y chromosome

How sex of baby is determined:
• Female produces only one type of gamete: 22 + X
• Male produces two types of gametes: 22 + X or 22 + Y

• If 22+X sperm fertilises egg (22+X) → 44+XX = Girl
• If 22+Y sperm fertilises egg (22+X) → 44+XY = Boy

Sex of the baby is determined by the father's gamete (X or Y sperm) — NOT the mother. Therefore, blaming the mother for the sex of a child has no scientific basis.
Q7. What are homologous and analogous organs? Give examples.
Homologous organs:
Organs that are similar in structure and origin (same evolutionary origin) but perform different functions in different organisms.
• Same basic bone structure (humerus, radius, ulna, carpals)
• Examples: Forelimb of human (grasping), whale (swimming), bat (flying), horse (running)
• Evidence of divergent evolution — common ancestor, different adaptations

Analogous organs:
Organs that perform similar functions but have different structures and origins.
• Examples: Wings of a bird vs wings of an insect; eyes of octopus vs eyes of vertebrates
• Evidence of convergent evolution — different ancestors, similar environmental pressures

Memory tip: Homo = same structure; Analog = same function
Q8. What are fossils? How are they formed? What is their importance?
Fossils are preserved remains or impressions of organisms that once existed on Earth.
The science of studying fossils is called Palaeontology.

Formation of fossils:
1. Organism dies and soft parts decay; hard parts (bones, shells) remain
2. Hard parts get buried under sediments of sand
3. Sediment layers accumulate over millions of years
4. Hard parts absorb minerals from surroundings → become mineralised
5. Sedimentary rock forms around them → fossils preserved in rock layers

Importance of fossils:
1. Tell us about types of life forms that existed in the past
2. Show how living things have changed over time (evolutionary evidence)
3. Indicate the time period when a life form existed (via radioactive dating)
4. Provide evidence for evolutionary relationships between species
Q9. What are the causes of evolution?
1. Natural Selection (Darwin's theory):
Process where individuals better adapted to their environment survive and reproduce more successfully. Traits that improve survival are passed on; unfavourable traits are eliminated over time.

2. Genetic Drift:
Accidental/random change in the frequency of alleles in a small population. Not driven by adaptation — purely by chance. Can cause traits to become common or disappear regardless of their usefulness.

3. Geographical Isolation:
When two sub-populations of the same species are separated by a geographical barrier (river, mountain, ocean), they cannot interbreed. They accumulate different mutations and adaptations over generations → eventually become different species (speciation).

Speciation = formation of new species from existing ones through accumulated changes.
Q10. What is the relationship between evolution and classification?
Evolution and classification are closely interlinked:

• Classification is based on shared characteristics
• The more characteristics two species share, the more closely they are related
• Species with recent common ancestors share more characteristics
• We can group species → supergroups with more distant common ancestors

Key principle: More shared characteristics = more recent common ancestor = closer evolutionary relationship

Example: Chimpanzees share ~98.7% of DNA with humans — they are our closest living relatives. Both descended from a common ancestor.

Evolution of eye (example of increasing complexity):
1. Simple photosensitive eyespot (flatworms)
2. Pit eyes — light/dark discrimination (nautilus)
3. Compound eyes — thousands of units (insects)
4. Highly complex camera-type eye (humans)
Q11. What does 'evolution is not always progress' mean? What is the origin of humans?
Evolution ≠ Progress:
Evolution simply creates more complex body designs — but simpler body designs are NOT inefficient or inferior.
• Bacteria have the simplest body design yet are the most numerous organisms on Earth
• Humans are only one branch of evolution — NOT the "highest" or "most evolved" species
• Every surviving species is equally "evolved" for its own niche

Human origin:
• All modern humans belong to a single species: Homo sapiens
• We all come from Africa — modern humans originated in Africa ~200,000 years ago
• Some ancestors migrated out of Africa; others stayed
• Migrants gradually spread across the entire planet
• There is no biological basis for "human races" — all humans are one species with minor surface variations
