Chapter Overview

The Science of Electric Charge in Motion

Electricity is the backbone of modern technology. At its core, it is simply the ordered flow of charged particles — electrons — through a conductor. Understanding electricity means understanding the rules that govern how charge flows, what opposes it, how it does work, and how that work becomes heat or light.

This chapter builds from first principles: what a charge is, what makes it flow (potential difference), what opposes flow (resistance), and how these are linked through Ohm's Law. We then explore how resistors combine in circuits, the heating effect of current (Joule's Law), and the commercial measurement of electrical energy.

Three Fundamental Quantities

Every concept in electricity traces back to these three quantities. Understand them precisely — their definitions, units, and relationships — and the rest of the chapter follows naturally.

q
Electric Charge
A fundamental property of matter. Electrons carry negative charge (−e), protons carry positive charge (+e). Charge is the source of all electrical phenomena.
Q = ne

Rate of flow of electric charge through a cross-section of a conductor. Flows from +ve to −ve terminal conventionally (opposite to electron flow).

02Ohm's Law & Resistance

The Central Law of Circuits

Ohm's Law, Resistance & Resistivity

Georg Simon Ohm
1787 – 1854
Discovery of Ohm's Law
German physicist Georg Simon Ohm conducted systematic experiments using a circuit with a battery, resistor, ammeter, and voltmeter. He varied the potential difference and measured the resulting current, plotting V against I. The graph was always a straight line through the origin — proving a constant ratio V/I = R for any given conductor at constant temperature. This relationship, published in 1827, is now known as Ohm's Law.
Ohm's Law — Mathematical Statement
V = I × R
V ∝ I at constant temperature

IElectric CurrentAmpere (A)
RResistance (constant)Ohm (Ω)
1ΩWhen 1V causes 1A to flowdefinition
V-I Graph: The graph between V (y-axis) and I (x-axis) is a straight line through the origin for an ohmic conductor. The slope of the graph = R. A steeper slope = higher resistance. This linearity holds only when temperature is constant.

03Series & Parallel Circuits
4
Combining Resistors

Series & Parallel Connections

Resistors can be connected in two fundamental ways. The mode of connection determines how current and voltage are distributed, and what the effective total resistance becomes.

Series vs Parallel — Circuit Diagrams & Effective Resistance Formulae
Maximum Resistance
Series Connection
R_eff = R₁ + R₂ + R₃
Same current I flows through all resistors
Voltage divides: V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃
Effective resistance is always greater than any individual resistor
Disadvantage: If one component fails, entire circuit breaks
Different appliances need different currents — series can't provide this
Used in: Christmas lights, fuse connections
Minimum Resistance
Parallel Connection
1/R_eff = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃
Same voltage V across all resistors
Current divides: I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃
Effective resistance is always less than the smallest individual resistor
Advantage: If one fails, others continue to work independently
Each appliance gets full voltage and draws its own required current
Used in: Home wiring, electrical appliances
04Heating Effect & Joule's Law
5
Electrical Energy → Heat

Joule's Law of Heating

When current flows through a resistor, electrons collide with the atoms of the conductor, transferring kinetic energy to them. This energy appears as heat. The battery keeps supplying energy to maintain the flow. This is the heating effect of electric current.

Joule's Law of Heating — Derivation
W = VQ = VIt
Work done = Potential difference × Charge moved
Since Q = It → W = VIt
V = IR (Ohm's Law) → substituting:
H = I²Rt
H — heat produced (Joules)
I² — square of current (most important factor)
R — resistance of conductor (Ω)
t — time for which current flows (seconds)
Joule's Law Summary: H ∝ I² (doubling current → 4× the heat) · H ∝ R · H ∝ t · This is why fuses, heating coils, and bulb filaments all rely on the heating effect — the difference is in how much heat they're designed to tolerate.
Electric Iron / Heater
Nichrome coil has high resistivity — generates intense heat when current flows. H = I²Rt ensures sustained heating at the operating current.
Alloy used: Nichrome (Ni + Cr). High ρ, doesn't oxidise.
Electric Bulb
Tungsten filament reaches ~2700°C, emitting visible light. High melting point (3380°C) prevents the filament from melting at operating temperature.
Filament: Tungsten. Inert gas (N₂/Ar) filled to reduce evaporation.
Electric Fuse
Low melting point alloy wire (Al, Cu, Fe, Pb). When excess current flows, I²Rt raises temperature, melting the fuse wire and breaking the circuit.
Always in series. Ratings: 1A, 2A, 3A, 5A, 10A. Protects appliances.
05Electric Power & Energy
6
Rate of Energy Consumption

Electric Power, Units & Billing

Electric Power is the rate at which electrical energy is consumed or dissipated in a circuit. It can be expressed in three equivalent forms using Ohm's Law.

P = VI
Voltage × Current
Most general form. Used when both V and I are known.
P = I²R
Current² × Resistance
Used when current and resistance are known. From V = IR.
P = V²/R
Voltage² ÷ Resistance
Used when voltage and resistance are known. From I = V/R.
SI vs Commercial Unit
Electrical Energy
SI Unit: Joule (J) or Watt-second (Ws) — E = Pt
Commercial Unit: Kilowatt-hour (kWh) — also called "1 unit"
1 kWh = 1000W × 3600s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J
Bills calculate: Units consumed = P(kW) × time(h)
1 Horsepower = 746 W (non-SI but still used)
Watt — SI Unit of Power
Power Definitions
1 Watt: Power consumed when 1A flows at 1V potential difference
P = 1W when: V = 1V and I = 1A (since P = VI)
kW = 10³ W  ·  MW = 10⁶ W
Fuse example: 1kW iron at 220V → I = 1000/220 ≈ 4.54A → use 5A fuse
2kW appliance at 220V → I = 9.09A → use 10A fuse
Quick Fuse Rule: Calculate I = P/V for the appliance. Choose a fuse with a rating just above that current. A 1kW, 220V appliance draws 4.54A → 5A fuse. A 2kW, 220V appliance draws 9.09A → 10A fuse, NOT 5A.

"Electricity is invisible — you cannot see an electron, you cannot watch current flow. What you can do is measure the voltage, the current, the resistance, and from those three numbers reconstruct the entire story of what is happening inside the wire."

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Define the SI units of current, potential difference, resistance, electric power, and commercial energy.
Current (I): Ampere (A) — 1A = 1 coulomb of charge flowing per second.

Potential Difference (V): Volt (V) — 1V = 1 joule of work done per coulomb of charge.

Resistance (R): Ohm (Ω) — 1Ω = resistance when 1V causes 1A to flow.

Electric Power (P): Watt (W) — 1W = 1 joule of energy consumed per second.

Commercial Energy: Kilowatt-hour (kWh) — 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. Also called "one unit" of electricity.
Q2. What is the conventional direction of flow of current?
Conventionally, electric current flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal of a battery — i.e., from higher potential to lower potential in the external circuit.

This is opposite to the actual direction of flow of electrons (which flow from negative to positive terminal). The conventional direction was established before electrons were discovered, and it has been retained for historical and practical reasons.
Q3. Define resistivity. What are its SI units?
Resistivity (ρ) is the resistance offered by a conductor of unit length and unit cross-sectional area. It is a property of the material itself, independent of its dimensions.

From the formula: R = ρl/A
ρ = RA/l

SI unit: Ω·m (ohm-metre)

Key facts: Resistivity increases with temperature for metals. Alloys generally have higher resistivity than pure metals. Resistivity of alloys is nearly constant over a range of temperatures — making them suitable for heating elements (e.g., Nichrome, Constantan).
Q4. On what factors does the resistance of a conductor depend?
Resistance of a conductor depends on:

1. Length (l): R ∝ l — longer wire has more resistance (more collisions)
2. Cross-sectional Area (A): R ∝ 1/A — thicker wire has less resistance (more paths for electrons)
3. Nature of Material: R = ρl/A — ρ (resistivity) depends on material
4. Temperature: Resistance increases with temperature for metals (more atomic vibrations)

Combined formula: R = ρl/A where ρ = resistivity of the material.
Q5. How is the voltmeter and ammeter connected in an electric circuit?
Ammeter: Always connected in series in the circuit. It measures current flowing through the circuit. An ammeter has very low resistance so that it does not significantly alter the current in the circuit.

Voltmeter: Always connected in parallel across the two points between which potential difference is to be measured. It has very high resistance so that negligible current flows through it, and it does not disturb the potential difference being measured.
Q6. State Ohm's Law and draw the V-I graph.
Ohm's Law: The electric current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across its ends, provided the temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.

Mathematically: V ∝ I → V = IR

Where R is the resistance (constant for a given conductor at constant temperature).

V-I Graph: A straight line passing through the origin. The slope of the graph = R (resistance). A steeper slope means higher resistance. This linearity is only valid when temperature is constant.
Q7. What is the heating effect of electric current? Derive Joule's Law.
When current flows through a resistor, electrons collide with atoms, converting electrical energy to heat. This is the heating effect of electric current.

Derivation:
Work done to move charge Q through potential difference V: W = VQ
Since Q = It: W = VIt
Using V = IR (Ohm's Law): W = (IR)It = H = I²Rt

Joule's Law: Heat produced H is:
• Proportional to I² (square of current)
• Proportional to R (resistance)
• Proportional to t (time)

Applications: Electric iron, heater, toaster, oven, electric bulb filament, fuse wire.
Q8. Why does the filament of a bulb have a high melting point?
The filament of a bulb is made of Tungsten because of its very high melting point of 3380°C.

When current flows through the filament, it heats up due to the heating effect of electric current. For the filament to glow and emit visible light, it must reach a very high temperature (around 2500–3000°C).

If the filament had a low melting point, it would melt and break before reaching the temperature needed to emit light. Tungsten retains structural integrity at these extreme temperatures, making it ideal for the purpose.
Q9. How does a fuse wire protect electrical appliances?
A fuse is a safety device made of an alloy with low melting point and high resistivity (e.g., alloys of Al, Cu, Fe, Pb).

Working principle: The fuse is always connected in series. When a fault causes excessive current to flow, the fuse wire heats up rapidly (H = I²Rt). Since its melting point is low, it melts quickly, breaking the circuit before the excess current can damage the appliances.

Common ratings: 1A, 2A, 3A, 5A, 10A — chosen based on the power rating of the appliance. A 1kW appliance at 220V draws ~4.54A → needs a 5A fuse.
Q10. Derive the formula for effective resistance in series and parallel.
Series: Same current I flows through all. Different voltage across each.
V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ = IR₁ + IR₂ + IR₃ = I(R₁+R₂+R₃)
R_eff = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ (maximum)

Parallel: Same voltage V across all. Different current through each.
I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ = V/R₁ + V/R₂ + V/R₃ = V(1/R₁+1/R₂+1/R₃)
1/R_eff = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ (minimum effective resistance)

Why parallel is used at home: Each appliance gets full voltage; if one fails, others continue to work; different appliances can draw different currents.
Q11. A wire of length L and resistance R is stretched so length is doubled and area halved. How do resistance and resistivity change?
Original: R = ρL/A

After stretching: New length = 2L, New area = A/2
New R' = ρ(2L)/(A/2) = ρ × 2L × 2/A = 4ρL/A = 4R

Resistance becomes 4 times the original.

Resistivity: Resistivity (ρ) is an intrinsic property of the material — it depends only on the nature of the material and temperature, NOT on shape or dimensions. So resistivity remains unchanged after stretching.
Q12. An appliance of 2kW works at 220V. Does it require a fuse of 5A?
Given: P = 2 kW = 2000 W, V = 220 V

Using P = VI:
I = P/V = 2000/220 = 9.09 A

Since the appliance draws ~9.1A, a 5A fuse is NOT sufficient — it would blow even during normal operation.

A fuse rated at 10A or higher should be used. The fuse rating must be slightly higher than the normal operating current, but low enough to blow during an overload or short circuit.
