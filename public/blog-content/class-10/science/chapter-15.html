Chapter Overview

Everything is Connected

It also covers waste management — how we classify, manage, and reduce waste to protect our environment for future generations.

01Waste Management
1
Types of Waste

Biodegradable vs Non-biodegradable

Waste is any unwanted, unused, or rejected material. It can be categorised by source (municipal, agricultural, industrial) or by type: solid, liquid, or gaseous.

✅ Breaks Down
Biodegradable Waste
Waste broken down by biological processes — microbes release enzymes to digest organic matter.
Source: Mainly plant and animal origin
Decomposed by bacteria and fungi using enzymes
Examples: food scraps, paper, cotton, wood, vegetable peels, dung
Converts to compost — useful for soil
Disposes naturally over time without harm
⚠️ Persists
Non-biodegradable Waste
Waste that cannot be broken down biologically. Persists for decades or centuries in the environment.
Source: Usually man-made/synthetic
Cannot be decomposed by microbes
Examples: plastic, metal, glass, DDT, synthetic fibres, nuclear waste
Accumulates in food chains (biomagnification)
Causes long-term environmental damage

The 7 principles of solid waste management help reduce environmental impact:

♻️
Recycle
Convert waste into reusable material — paper, glass, metals
🔁
Reuse
Use items again instead of discarding — bottles, bags
📉
Reduce
Use less — cut consumption of materials that create waste
🚫
Refuse
Say no to unnecessary items — single-use plastics
💭
Rethink
Change habits — think before buying or discarding
🔬
Research
Find better biodegradable alternatives to harmful materials
📋
Regulation
Government laws + public awareness to enforce waste management
🗑️ Separate bins: Blue bin = non-biodegradable waste · Green bin = biodegradable waste. Separation at source is the most effective waste management practice.
02Environment & Ecosystem

Ecological Organisation

Ecological Hierarchy

Environment is the natural surroundings and external conditions that include all living and non-living factors affecting an organism. Life is organised in a hierarchy from individual organisms to the entire biosphere.

🦠Organism
Basic unit of ecological hierarchy. Can be unicellular (Amoeba, Paramecium) or multicellular (humans, trees). Smallest ecological unit.
🐘Population
A group of individuals of the same species inhabiting a given geographical area at a particular time and functioning as a unit. E.g., all deer in a forest.
🌿Community
All individuals of different species living within a certain geographical area. Includes all populations in the same location. E.g., all plants, animals, fungi in a forest.
🌍Ecosystem
Includes both living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) components of an area interacting together. E.g., a pond, a forest, a desert.
🌐Biosphere
The sum total of all ecosystems on Earth and their interactions. The global ecological system integrating all living beings and their relationships.
Non-living
Abiotic Factors
Light — energy source for photosynthesis
Temperature — affects metabolic rates of organisms
Water — essential for all life processes
Air — provides O₂ and CO₂ for respiration and photosynthesis
Soil — provides minerals and anchoring for plants

Living organisms
Biotic Factors
Autotrophs/Producers — make own food (plants, algae)
Herbivores — eat only plants (deer, grasshopper)
Carnivores — eat animals (frog, eagle, cat)
Omnivores — eat both plants and animals (bear, humans)
Decomposers — break down dead matter; recycle nutrients (bacteria, fungi)
Decomposers are essential for nutrient cycling

⚡ Lindemann's 10% Law of Energy Transfer
Only 10% Energy Passes to the Next Level
At each trophic level, 90% of energy is lost as heat through metabolic processes. Only 10% is transferred to the next trophic level. This is why food chains are short — too little energy remains beyond 3–4 levels.

⚠️
Biomagnification — Pollutants Concentrate Up the Food Chain

Biomagnification is the increase in concentration of non-biodegradable pollutants (DDT, mercury, PCBs) at each successive trophic level. Each organism retains the pollutant in its fat tissues. As predators eat many prey, concentrations multiply dramatically up the chain.

⚠️ Humans as top consumers can accumulate the highest concentrations — causing cancer, reproductive failure, neurological damage.

Ozone Depletion & CFCs

🌐 The Ozone Layer — Earth's UV Shield
🌍
What is Ozone (O₃)?
Ozone is a protective gas in the stratosphere (15–35 km above Earth) that absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the Sun. Sunlight reaching Earth has UV, visible, and infrared components — ozone blocks the damaging UV. In recent years, ozone has been rapidly depleted mainly due to Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs / freons) — used in refrigerants and aerosols.
How CFCs Destroy Ozone
(i)CFd3+ hν →CFd2+Cl•UV breaks CFC → Cl atom
(ii)Cl+O3→ClO+O2Cl destroys ozone
1 Cl atom can destroy ~100,000 ozone molecules
Ozone hole most prominent over Antarctic region
CFCs (freons) used as refrigerants in AC, fridges, aerosol sprays
UV light in stratosphere releases Cl atoms from CFCs
Consequences of Ozone Depletion
Skin darkening — increased UV exposure causes melanin overproduction
Skin cancer — UV radiation damages DNA in skin cells
Premature ageing — UV breaks down collagen in skin
Corneal cataracts — eye lens damage from UV
Death of phytoplankton — disrupts entire aquatic food chains
Reduced crop yields; harm to terrestrial ecosystems

"A single chlorine atom released from CFCs can destroy 100,000 ozone molecules. The ozone hole over Antarctica is visible from satellites — a direct consequence of decades of chemical pollution from human activity."

🌐 Montreal Protocol (1987) — international treaty to phase out CFC production. One of the most successful environmental agreements — the ozone layer is slowly recovering. Estimated full recovery by 2060–2070.
Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What are biodegradable and non-biodegradable wastes? Give examples.
Biodegradable wastes:
Wastes that can be broken down by biological processes (decomposed by microbes using enzymes).
• Mainly produced from plant and animal sources
• Examples: food scraps, vegetable peels, paper, cotton, wood, dead leaves, dung
• Microbes digest and consume this organic waste

Non-biodegradable wastes:
Wastes that cannot be broken down by biological processes.
• Usually man-made substances
• Examples: plastic, metal, glass, DDT, synthetic fibres, nuclear waste
• Persist in the environment for very long periods
• Often toxic and accumulate in food chains (biomagnification)

Waste management: Use blue bins for non-biodegradable, green bins for biodegradable. Follow the 7 Rs: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Refuse, Rethink, Research, Regulation.
Q2. What is the ecological hierarchy? Define each level.
Ecological hierarchy (from smallest to largest):

1. Organism — basic unit; can be unicellular (Amoeba, Paramecium) or multicellular (humans, trees)
2. Population — group of individuals of the SAME species in a geographical area at a particular time
3. Community — all individuals of DIFFERENT species living in the same geographical area
4. Ecosystem — both living (biotic) AND non-living (abiotic) components of an area interacting together
5. Biosphere — sum total of ALL ecosystems on Earth and their interactions

Key distinction: Population = same species; Community = different species
Q3. What are the components of an ecosystem? Distinguish between biotic and abiotic factors.
Abiotic factors — non-living components:
Light, temperature, water, air, soil, minerals, humidity, pH

Biotic factors — living organisms, classified by feeding:
• Autotrophs/Producers — make own food from inorganic raw materials (plants, algae) — 1st trophic level
• Herbivores/Primary consumers — feed on plants (deer, grasshopper, sheep) — 2nd trophic level
• Carnivores/Secondary consumers — eat herbivores (frog, cat, spider) — 3rd trophic level
• Tertiary consumers — eat secondary consumers (eagle, shark) — 4th trophic level
• Omnivores — eat both plants and animals (bear, humans)
• Decomposers — break down dead organic matter; recycle nutrients (bacteria, fungi)

Functions of ecosystem: Productivity (organic matter production) and Decomposition/recycling of nutrients
Q4. What is a food chain? What is a food web?
Food chain: A linear sequence of organisms where each organism is eaten by the next member.
Energy flows from producers → consumers in one direction.

Example:
Plants → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle

Generalised food chain:
Producers → Primary consumers → Secondary consumers → Tertiary consumers → Decomposers

Food web: An interconnected network of multiple food chains in an ecosystem. More realistic than a single food chain — most organisms eat more than one type of food.

Trophic level: The position or level of an organism in a food chain based on its source of nutrition. Producers = 1st trophic level; herbivores = 2nd trophic level, etc.
Q5. Explain the 10% law of energy transfer. Why are food chains short?
10% Law (Lindemann's Law):
Only 10% of the energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next higher trophic level. The remaining 90% is lost as heat during metabolic processes.

Example:
• Producers absorb: 1,000,000 J (1000 kJ)
• Primary consumers get: 10% = 100,000 J
• Secondary consumers get: 10% = 10,000 J
• Tertiary consumers get: 10% = 1,000 J

Why food chains are short:
Since only 10% energy passes at each level, by the 4th or 5th trophic level the available energy is so small that it cannot sustain a population. Therefore, most food chains have only 3–4 trophic levels.

Pyramid of energy: Graphical representation of energy exchange in the ecosystem — always upright (energy decreases upward).
Q6. What is biomagnification? Why is it harmful?
Biomagnification (biological magnification) is the increase in concentration of harmful chemicals or pollutants at each successive trophic level in a food chain.

How it happens:
• Non-biodegradable chemicals (like DDT, mercury) are absorbed by organisms but cannot be broken down
• They accumulate in the fatty tissues
• Each time one organism eats another, the chemical concentrations multiply

Example (DDT in water → aquatic food chain):
Water (0.0001 ppm) → Phytoplankton (0.04 ppm) → Small fish (0.5 ppm) → Large fish (2 ppm) → Birds (25 ppm)

Why harmful: Top predators (including humans) accumulate the highest concentrations — causing reproductive failure, nervous system damage, cancer, and other diseases.
Q7. What is the ozone layer? Why is it important?
Ozone (O₃) is a molecule made of three oxygen atoms. It forms a protective layer in the stratosphere (15–35 km above Earth).

Importance:
• Absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the Sun
• Without it, UV radiation would reach Earth's surface causing widespread damage

Sunlight components reaching Earth:
• Ultraviolet radiation (harmful — absorbed by ozone)
• Visible radiation (useful — we can see it)
• Infrared radiation (heat energy)

Ozone formation:
UV radiation splits O₂ → O atoms, which combine with O₂ → O₃ (ozone)
Q8. How is the ozone layer being depleted? What are the consequences?
Cause of ozone depletion:
Mainly due to Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) — also called freons. Used as refrigerants in ACs, refrigerators, and in aerosol sprays.

Mechanism:
1. CFCs rise to the stratosphere
2. UV light breaks CFCs: CFd₃ + hν → CFd₂ + d (Cl atom)
3. Cl atom destroys ozone: Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂
4. One Cl atom can destroy ~100,000 ozone molecules!
5. This creates the ozone hole — most prominent over Antarctica

Consequences of ozone depletion:
• Skin darkening, skin cancer, premature aging
• Corneal cataracts (eye damage)
• Death of phytoplankton → disruption of aquatic food chains
• Reduced crop yields and harm to ecosystems
