Carbon is the 6th element in the periodic table — yet it forms more compounds than all other elements combined. From the graphite in your pencil to the DNA in your cells, carbon is the backbone of all life and organic chemistry.

This chapter covers covalent bonding, allotropes, catenation, hydrocarbons, functional groups, IUPAC nomenclature, chemical reactions of carbon compounds, and soaps & detergents.

1
Electron Sharing

Covalent Bonds & Allotropes

Covalent bonds are formed by the mutual sharing of electrons. Both atoms contribute and both own the shared electrons. Carbon (configuration 2,4) has 4 valence electrons — losing or gaining 4 is energetically impossible, so it always shares, forming exactly 4 covalent bonds.

⚛️
Carbon's Valency
Why Carbon Always Forms 4 Bonds
Carbon (atomic no. 6, configuration 2,4) has 4 valence electrons. Losing all 4 → C⁴⁺ (needs enormous energy). Gaining 4 → C⁴⁻ (highly unstable). The only option: share all 4 with other atoms through covalent bonds. This gives carbon its extraordinary ability to form chains, rings, and millions of compounds.

Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element — same chemistry, vastly different physical properties. Carbon has three main allotropes:

Chemical Reactions of Carbon Compounds

Carbon compounds undergo four main types of reactions. Each has a different mechanism and produces different products.

🔥
Reaction 01
Combustion
Carbon compound + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + Heat

All carbon compounds burn in excess O₂ to give CO₂, H₂O, heat and light. Incomplete combustion → CO (toxic) or soot.

Addition Reaction
Unsaturated + H₂ → Saturated

Unsaturated hydrocarbons react with H₂ in presence of nickel catalyst → saturated hydrocarbons. Called hydrogenation. Used to make vanaspati ghee from vegetable oils.

Cleansing Agents

Soaps & Detergents

Soaps are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids. Prepared by heating oils/fats with NaOH (saponification), then adding NaCl to precipitate the soap (salting out).

Detergents are ammonium or sulphonate salts of long hydrocarbon chains. Prepared by treating petroleum hydrocarbons with conc. H₂SO₄ then NaOH.

🧼 Cleansing Action — How Micelles Work
Soap molecules have two distinct ends. When soap is added to dirty water, the hydrophobic tails embed into oil/grease while hydrophilic heads face outward toward water. This forms a spherical cluster called a micelle. The trapped grease stays emulsified and is washed away with rinsing water.
Hydrophilic Head (Ionic)
Water-loving. The carboxylate/ionic end. Points outward into water in the micelle.
Hydrophobic Tail (Hydrocarbon)
Water-fearing. Long hydrocarbon chain. Points inward into the oil droplet.
⚠️ Soaps fail in hard water — Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ salts in hard water react with soap to form insoluble scum (calcium/magnesium stearate), wasting soap and leaving residue on fabric.

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Why does carbon always form covalent bonds?
Carbon has 4 valence electrons (configuration 2,4). To form an ionic bond it would have to either lose all 4 electrons (forming C⁴⁺ — requires enormous energy) or gain 4 electrons (forming C⁴⁻ — very unstable). Neither is feasible.

So carbon achieves stability by sharing electrons with other atoms, forming covalent bonds. It can form 4 covalent bonds simultaneously — with H, O, N, S, halogens, or other carbon atoms.
Q2. What is catenation? Why is it special to carbon?
Catenation is the ability of an element to bond with its own atoms through covalent bonds to form long chains.

Carbon shows extraordinary catenation because C–C bonds are very strong and stable. Carbon can form:
• Straight chains — e.g., n-butane
• Branched chains — e.g., 2-methylpropane
• Ring structures — e.g., cyclohexane, benzene
• Single, double, and triple bonds with itself

Silicon also shows limited catenation, but no other element does it as extensively as carbon — which is why millions of carbon compounds exist.
Q3. What are allotropes? Compare diamond, graphite and buckminsterfullerene.
Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element — chemically identical but physically very different.

Diamond: Each C bonds to 4 others in a rigid 3D tetrahedral lattice. Hardest natural substance. Does NOT conduct electricity. Used in cutting tools and jewellery.

Graphite: C atoms in hexagonal layers, each C bonded to 3 others. Layers slide — soft and slippery. Conducts electricity (one free e⁻ per atom). Used in pencils, lubricants, electrodes.

Buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀): 60 C atoms forming a hollow sphere of 20 hexagons + 12 pentagons. Named after architect Buckminster Fuller. Used in nanotechnology.

Amorphous forms: Charcoal, coke, coal, lampblack — no regular crystal structure.
Q4. What is the difference between saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons?
Saturated hydrocarbons (Alkanes):
• Contain only single bonds (C–C) between carbons
• General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
• Example: Methane (CH₄), Ethane (C₂H₆), Propane (C₃H₈)
• Less reactive — undergo substitution reactions

Unsaturated hydrocarbons:
• Alkenes — at least one double bond (C=C); formula CₙH₂ₙ — e.g., Ethene (C₂H₄)
• Alkynes — at least one triple bond (C≡C); formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ — e.g., Ethyne (C₂H₂)
• More reactive — can undergo addition reactions
Q5. What is a functional group? What is a homologous series?
Functional group: An atom or group of atoms that determines the characteristic chemical properties of an organic compound.

Key groups: –OH (alcohol), –CHO (aldehyde), >C=O (ketone), –COOH (carboxylic acid), –Cl/–Br (halo group).

Homologous series: A series of organic compounds that differ from consecutive members by –CH₂– (14 mass units), have the same functional group, and follow the same general formula. All members show similar chemical properties but gradually changing physical properties (e.g., increasing boiling point with chain length).

Example: CH₄ → C₂H₆ → C₃H₈ → C₄H₁₀ (alkane homologous series)
Q6. How do you name organic compounds using IUPAC nomenclature?
IUPAC naming follows these steps:

Step I — Select longest continuous C chain → gives root word: meth(1) eth(2) prop(3) but(4) pent(5) hex(6)
Step II — Number from end nearest to a branch or functional group (lowest locant).
Step III — Name and number substituents alphabetically as prefixes (e.g., 2-methyl, 3-chloro).
Step IV — Add suffix for functional group: -ane / -ene / -yne / -ol / -al / -one / -oic acid
Step V — Give functional group the lowest possible position number.
Step VI — Fill remaining valencies with hydrogen atoms.
Q7. What are the four types of chemical reactions of carbon compounds?
1. Combustion: Carbon compounds burn in O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O + heat & light.
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + Heat
Incomplete combustion gives CO (toxic) or soot (C).

2. Oxidation: Alcohols are oxidised to carboxylic acids using alkaline KMnO₄ or K₂Cr₂O₇.
CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOH (Alk. KMnO₄ / heat)

3. Addition: Unsaturated compounds + H₂ → saturated (Ni catalyst) — called hydrogenation. Used to make vanaspati ghee.
RCH=CHR + H₂ → RCH₂–CH₂R (Ni catalyst)

4. Substitution: H atoms in saturated hydrocarbons replaced by halogens in sunlight.
CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (sunlight)
Q8. What are the properties and chemical reactions of ethanol?
Ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) — ethyl alcohol.

Physical: Liquid at room temperature · good solvent · miscible with water in all proportions.

Chemical reactions:
• With Na metal: 2Na + 2CH₃CH₂OH → 2CH₃CH₂O⁻Na⁺ + H₂↑
• Dehydration (hot conc. H₂SO₄): CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O (forms ethene)
• Oxidation (KMnO₄): CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃COOH (ethanoic acid)

Uses: Antiseptic, solvent in medicines & perfumes, alcoholic beverages, fuel blending.
Q9. What is ethanoic acid? What are esterification and saponification?
Ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH) — common name: acetic acid. Dilute aqueous solution = vinegar.
Colourless, pungent-smelling liquid, miscible with water. It is a weak acid.

Esterification: Reaction of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol (acid catalyst) → ester + water. Esters have fruity fragrance.
CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH → CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O

Saponification: Hydrolysis of an ester in the presence of NaOH → alcohol + sodium carboxylate salt. This is the basis of soap making.

Other reactions: with NaOH → sodium ethanoate + water; with Na₂CO₃ → CO₂ gas evolved.
Q10. How are soaps made? Why do soaps not work in hard water?
Soap preparation: Fats/oils are heated with NaOH (saponification). NaCl is added to precipitate the soap out of solution.

Why soaps fail in hard water:
Hard water contains Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ salts. When soap is added, it reacts with these ions to form insoluble calcium/magnesium stearate — scum. This wastes soap and leaves white residue on fabric.

Detergents vs Soaps:
• Detergents work in hard water — no scum formation
• Detergents are more soluble and have stronger cleansing action
• Detergents work in acidic medium; soaps do not
• Soaps are biodegradable; many detergents may not be
Q11. What is a micelle? How does soap remove oily dirt?
Soap molecules have two different ends:
• Hydrophilic head — the ionic carboxylate end; attracted to water
• Hydrophobic tail — the long hydrocarbon chain; repels water, attracted to oil/grease

When soap is mixed into dirty water:
1. Hydrophobic tails embed into the oil/grease droplet
2. Hydrophilic heads face outward into water
3. This forms a spherical cluster called a micelle
4. Grease is trapped inside the micelle — emulsified
5. Micelles stay suspended and are washed away with rinsing water

Soap removes grease that water alone cannot dissolve because water is polar and cannot interact with non-polar oil.
Q12. What is structural isomerism? Give examples.
Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements are called isomers; the phenomenon is isomerism.

Example: C₄H₁₀ has two isomers:
• n-Butane — straight chain C–C–C–C
• 2-Methylpropane — branched C–C(–C)–C

Types of structural isomerism:
• Chain isomerism — different carbon skeleton
• Position isomerism — different position of functional group or multiple bond
• Functional group isomerism — different functional groups (e.g., alcohol vs ether from C₂H₆O)
• Metamerism — different C atoms on either side of the functional group
