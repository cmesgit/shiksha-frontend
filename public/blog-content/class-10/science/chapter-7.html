// Chapter Overview

Control and coordination is the working together of various integrated body systems in response to changes in the surroundings — maintaining bodily functions and homeostasis. Without it, a living organism would simply fall apart.

This chapter covers the nervous system (neurons, brain, spinal cord, reflexes), the endocrine system (hormones from 9+ glands), and plant responses (tropic/nastic movements and phytohormones).

// Electrical Signals

The Neuron — Structure & Types

Neurons are the functional units of the nervous system — specialised cells that conduct messages as electrical and chemical impulses throughout the body.

⚡ Anatomy of a Neuron
🔵
Cell Body (Soma)
Contains the nucleus and cytoplasm. The metabolic centre of the neuron. All cellular functions originate here.
🌿
Dendrites
Short, branched extensions of the cell body. Receive incoming signals from other neurons or sensory receptors.
➡️
Axon
Long fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body to the next neuron or effector organ. Covered by myelin sheath for insulation.
⚡
Nerve Endings
Terminal branches at the end of the axon. Release neurotransmitters into the synapse to transmit impulses to the next cell.
👁️
Sensory Neuron
Also called afferent neuron
Carries impulses FROM receptors TO the CNS
Brings information about the environment inward
Examples: neurons in eyes, ears, skin receptors
💪
Motor Neuron
Also called efferent neuron
Carries impulses FROM CNS TO effectors
Effectors are muscles or glands that respond
Produces the visible response/action
🔗
Relay Neuron
Also called interneuron
Connects sensory and motor neurons
Located entirely within the CNS (brain/spinal cord)
Processes and integrates information

// Electrochemical Signal

Transmission of Nerve Impulse

⚡ How an Impulse Travels Along an Axon
🟢
RESTING STATE (Polarised)
Outside axon membrane has more Na⁺ ions → positive charge outside, negative inside. Neuron is in polarised (resting) state. No signal travelling.
⚡
STIMULATION (Depolarisation)
Stimulus arrives → membrane becomes permeable → Na⁺ ions rush inside → charge reverses. Outside becomes negative, inside positive. This region = excited/depolarised region.
🌊
PROPAGATION
The depolarised region acts as stimulus for the neighbouring region, which also depolarises. This wave of depolarisation travels along the axon from point to point.
🔁
REPOLARISATION
The previously excited area recovers: sodium pump uses ATP to actively transport Na⁺ back outside. Membrane returns to resting (polarised) state and is ready for the next impulse.
Gap Junction
What is a Synapse?
A synapse is the tiny gap between the axon terminal of one neuron and the dendrite of the next neuron. It is where the electrical signal becomes chemical.
Axon terminal releases chemical neurotransmitters into the gap
Neurotransmitters diffuse across the synapse
Receptors on the next dendrite bind them and regenerate the electrical impulse
Ensures one-way signal transmission
Medullary sheath insulates axons in a nerve, preventing signal mixing
Nerve vs Neuron
What is a Nerve?
A nerve is a collection of nerve fibres (axons) bundled together and enclosed in a tubular medullary sheath.
Medullary sheath acts as electrical insulation
Prevents mixing of impulses in adjacent fibres
One nerve can contain thousands of axons
Neuron = single cell (structural unit)
Nerve = bundle of many axons (anatomical structure)
🧠 Human nervous system is divided into: Central Nervous System (CNS) — brain + spinal cord, and Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) — all nerves connecting CNS to the rest of the body.

Reflex Action & Reflex Arc

A reflex action is an automatic, involuntary, rapid response to a stimulus — it bypasses the brain and is processed in the spinal cord, saving precious milliseconds.

Classic Example: You touch a hot plate → pain receptor in skin detects heat → sensory neuron carries signal to spinal cord → relay neuron processes → motor neuron fires → hand muscles contract and pull away — all before the brain even consciously registers the pain!
⚡ 3 types of responses: Reflex action — sudden, automatic, no brain involvement (e.g., touching hot object). Voluntary action — conscious, brain-controlled (e.g., writing, talking). Involuntary action — automatic, not consciously controlled (e.g., heartbeat, digestion).

Hormones & Endocrine Glands

Hormones are chemical secretions produced by endocrine glands, released directly into the bloodstream, and carried to specific target organs. Unlike the nervous system, hormonal responses are slower but longer-lasting.

📨
Chemical Messenger
Carry information from one organ to another via blood
💧
Tiny Quantities
Produced and effective in very small amounts
🎯
Target Specific
Act only on specific target cells, tissues or organs
⏱️
Slow & Long-lasting
Slow in action but effects persist for long duration
⚖️
Accelerate or Inhibit
Can speed up or slow down bodily reactions
🔄
Feedback Regulated
Pituitary controls production via positive/negative feedback

Pituitary Gland

Target gland produces hormone

Negative feedback — most common; high hormone level suppresses further production. Positive feedback — high level amplifies production (e.g., during childbirth). This self-regulating mechanism keeps hormone levels in precise balance.
🧠
Master Gland
Pituitary Gland
→ Growth Hormone (GH)
Pea-sized; located at base of the brain
Required for proper body growth and development
Hyposecretion of GH → Dwarfism (stunted growth)
Hypersecretion → Gigantism in children
Hypersecretion in adults → Acromegaly (enlarged extremities)
Controls all other endocrine glands — hence called the master gland
🦋

Thyroid Gland
→ Thyroxine
Located close to trachea in the neck; butterfly-shaped
Regulates metabolism — controls how fast body uses energy

Lack of iodine → thyroxine deficiency → goitre (enlarged thyroid)
Hypersecretion → Hyperthyroidism → high metabolism, bulging eyes, high BP, nervous tension
🦴
Calcium Regulator
Parathyroid Gland
→ Parathormone (PTH)
4 small glands on the back side of the thyroid gland
Regulates calcium ion levels in the bloodstream
Excess PTH → removes calcium from bones → bones become soft and fragile
Deficiency → muscle cramps, tetany
🍬
Sugar Regulator

→ Insulin + Glucagon
Both an endocrine and exocrine gland (mixed gland)
Insulin — lowers blood sugar; promotes glucose absorption into cells
Glucagon — raises blood sugar; promotes glucose release from liver

Together they maintain blood sugar homeostasis
🚨
Stress Gland
Adrenal Gland
→ Adrenaline + Cortisol
Two adrenal glands — one on top of each kidney
Cortex → Cortisol — regulates rate of metabolism, anti-inflammatory
Medulla → Adrenaline — fight-or-flight hormone; prepares body for stress

⚧

Ovaries (females) → Estrogen + Progesterone
Estrogen — female secondary characteristics; deficiency → infertility
Progesterone — prepares uterus for pregnancy, maintains it

"Adrenaline is the body's own emergency alarm system — in under a second it redirects blood from your stomach to your muscles, dilates your pupils, spikes your heart rate, and floods your bloodstream with glucose. Fight or flight, decided chemically."

// Plant Behaviour

Movement in Plants

Plants cannot move from place to place, but they respond to stimuli by showing movement of specific parts. These can be growth-dependent (tropic) or growth-independent (nastic).

Growth-dependent
🌱 Tropic Movement
Directional movement in response to stimulus. The direction of movement depends on the direction of the stimulus.
  • Involves growth — if growth stopped, movement stops
  • Direction of movement = direction of stimulus
  • Phototropism — towards/away from light
  • Geotropism — roots downward, shoots upward (gravity)
  • Hydrotropism — towards moisture
  • Chemotropism — towards chemicals (pollen tube)
  • Thigmotropism — in response to touch (tendrils)
Growth-independent
🌿 Nastic Movement
Response to environmental stimulus but direction of response is NOT determined by direction of stimulus.
  • Does NOT involve growth
  • Direction predetermined by plant structure
  • Caused by changes in turgor pressure in cells
  • Thigmonastic — Mimosa pudica (touch-me-not) folds leaves
  • Thermonastic — tulip flowers open/close with temperature
  • Photonastic — daisy opens in day, closes at night
  • Example: Mimosa pudica is growth-INDEPENDENT nastic movement
☀️

Plant Hormones

Growth and development in plants is regulated by chemical substances called phytohormones (plant hormones). They control everything from root growth to fruit ripening.

Hormone
Primary Function
Key Details
Auxin
Growth Promoter
Stem growth
Promotes growth of stem tip. Responsible for phototropism — unequal distribution causes bending toward light. Produced at shoot apex.
Gibberellin
Growth Promoter
Stem elongation
Promotes stem elongation, seed germination, and fruit development. Can cause plants to grow much taller.
Cytokinin
Growth Promoter
Cell division
Promotes cell division (cytokinesis). Delays ageing of leaves (senescence). Works with auxin to regulate growth.
Abscisic Acid
Growth Inhibitor
Dormancy & stress
Promotes seed dormancy; causes stomata to close during water stress; inhibits growth. Called the 'stress hormone'.
Ethylene
Ripening Agent
Fruit ripening
Only gaseous plant hormone. Regulates fruit ripening, promotes leaf/fruit/flower drop (abscission). Used commercially to ripen bananas.

// Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is control and coordination? Which systems provide it in animals?
Working together of various integrated body systems in response to changes in the surroundings for the maintenance of bodily functions is called control and coordination.

In animals, it is provided by two systems:
• Nervous system — uses electrical and chemical impulses for fast, short-duration responses
• Endocrine system — uses chemical messengers (hormones) for slow, long-lasting responses
Q2. What is a neuron? Describe its structure and types.
Neurons are the functional units of the nervous system. They conduct messages as electrical and chemical impulses.

Structure:
• Cell body (Soma) — contains nucleus and cytoplasm
• Dendrites — short branched extensions that receive impulses
• Axon — long fibre that carries impulses away from cell body
• Nerve endings — transmit impulses to next neuron or effector

Types of neurons:
• Sensory (afferent) — carries impulses from receptors to CNS
• Motor (efferent) — carries impulses from CNS to effectors (muscles/glands)
• Relay (interneuron) — connects sensory and motor neurons within CNS
Q3. Explain the transmission of a nerve impulse. What is a synapse?
At rest (Polarised state): Outside of axon has more Na⁺ ions → positive charge outside, negative inside.

On stimulation (Depolarisation): Membrane becomes permeable → Na⁺ ions rush inside → charge reverses (negative outside, positive inside). This is the excited region.

Propagation: The depolarised region acts as a stimulus for the next region → impulse travels along the axon.

Repolarisation: Sodium pump uses ATP to push Na⁺ back outside → membrane returns to resting state.

Synapse: The tiny gap between the axon terminal of one neuron and the dendrite of the next neuron. Chemical neurotransmitters released at the synapse carry the signal across this gap.
Q4. Describe the human brain — its parts and functions.
Protection: Enclosed in cranium (skull). Brain + spinal cord covered by meninges (3 layers: duramater → arachnoid → piamater). Space filled with CSF (cerebrospinal fluid).

Forebrain (cerebrum + thalamus + hypothalamus):
• Thinking, intelligence, learning, memory, speech
• Sensory and motor regions; hypothalamus controls body temperature, hunger, thirst

Midbrain: Controls sight and hearing responses

Hindbrain (pons + medulla + cerebellum):
• Medulla controls involuntary actions — heartbeat, blood pressure, digestion, salivation
• Cerebellum controls voluntary movements, posture, and body equilibrium
Q5. What is a reflex action? Describe the reflex arc.
Reflex action: An automatic, involuntary, rapid response to a stimulus that does not involve conscious thought or the brain.

Reflex arc — pathway:
Receptor → Sensory neuron → Spinal cord (Association neuron) → Motor neuron → Effector

• Receptor — sense organ detects the stimulus
• Sensory neuron — carries impulse to spinal cord
• Association neuron — transmits from sensory to motor neuron within spinal cord
• Motor neuron — carries impulse to effector (muscle or gland)
• Effector — muscle or gland that responds

Example: Touching a hot plate → hand pulls back immediately (before brain processes pain).
Q6. What are hormones? What are their characteristics?
Hormones are chemical secretions produced by endocrine glands that bring about specific changes in the body.

Characteristics:
1. Act as chemical messengers
2. Secreted by living glands/cells/tissues
3. Secreted in very small quantities
4. Travel via blood to reach specific target sites
5. Slow in action but have long-lasting effects
6. Either accelerate or inhibit a reaction

Hormone production is regulated by a feedback mechanism (positive and negative) controlled by the pituitary gland.
Q7. Describe all the major endocrine glands and their hormones.
Pituitary — pea-sized, base of brain. Growth Hormone (GH). Hyposecretion → dwarfism; Hypersecretion → gigantism (children), acromegaly (adults).

Thyroid — near trachea in neck. Thyroxine. Regulates metabolism. Hypo → goitre, myxoedema, cretinism; Hyper → high metabolism, bulging eyes, high BP.

Parathyroid — 4 glands on back of thyroid. PTH. Regulates blood calcium. Excess PTH → bones become soft (calcium removed).

Pancreas — Insulin + Glucagon. Regulate blood sugar. Insulin deficiency → Diabetes mellitus.

Adrenal — above each kidney. Cortex → Cortisol (regulates metabolism). Medulla → Adrenaline (fight-or-flight response).

Gonads — Testes → Testosterone (puberty, male features). Ovaries → Estrogen (female features; deficiency → infertility) + Progesterone.
Q8. What is adrenaline? Why is it called the 'fight-or-flight' hormone?
Adrenaline (epinephrine) is a hormone secreted by the medulla of the adrenal gland in response to stress, danger, or excitement.

Effects on the body during stress:
• Heart rate increases — pumps more blood to muscles
• Breathing rate increases — more oxygen intake
• Blood glucose rises — more energy available
• Blood diverted from digestive system to muscles
• Pupils dilate — better vision
• Non-essential functions slow down

All these effects prepare the body to either fight the threat or flee from it — hence the name "fight-or-flight hormone". It acts very rapidly unlike most other hormones.
Q9. Explain tropic movements in plants with examples.
Tropic movements are directional movements of a specific part of a plant in response to an external stimulus. The direction of movement depends on the direction of the stimulus.

• Phototropism — response to light. Shoot grows towards light (positive); root grows away (negative). Caused by unequal distribution of auxin.
• Geotropism — response to gravity. Root grows downward (positive geotropism); shoot grows upward (negative geotropism). Seen during seed germination.
• Hydrotropism — response to water/moisture. Roots grow towards water.
• Chemotropism — response to chemicals. Pollen tube grows towards ovule through chemical signals.
• Thigmotropism — response to touch. Tendrils of climbing plants coil around support.

Tropic movements involve growth — if growth is stopped, the movement stops.
Q10. What are nastic movements? How are they different from tropic movements?
Nastic movements occur in response to environmental stimuli, but unlike tropic movements, the direction of the response is NOT dependent on the direction of the stimulus.

Comparison:
• Tropic → direction determined by stimulus direction
• Nastic → direction pre-determined by plant structure, not stimulus direction

Types of nastic movements:
• Thigmonastic — response to touch (e.g., Mimosa pudica / touch-me-not folds leaves when touched)
• Thermonastic — response to temperature changes (e.g., tulip flowers open/close with temperature)
• Photonastic — response to light intensity (e.g., daisy flowers open in day, close at night)

The movement of Mimosa pudica is a growth-independent nastic movement — it does not involve growth, unlike tropic movements.
Q11. What are plant hormones (phytohormones)? Name them and give their functions.
Phytohormones are chemical substances produced naturally in plants that regulate growth and development.

1. Auxin — promotes growth of stem; responsible for phototropism (unequal distribution causes bending). Produced at shoot tip.
2. Gibberellin — promotes stem elongation; helps in seed germination and fruit development.
3. Cytokinin — promotes cell division (cytokinesis); delays ageing of leaves.
4. Abscisic Acid (ABA) — promotes seed dormancy; causes stomata to close; inhibits growth (also called the "stress hormone").
5. Ethylene — regulates fruit ripening; promotes fruit drop and leaf fall.

Growth hormones: Auxin, Gibberellin, Cytokinin (promoters). Inhibitor: Abscisic acid.
Q12. Differentiate between growth-dependent and growth-independent movements in plants.
Growth-dependent movement:
• Movement is caused by differential growth of plant parts
• If growth is prevented, movement stops
• Examples: phototropism, geotropism (seed germination — root grows down, shoot grows up)
• These are tropic movements

Growth-independent movement:
• Movement is NOT caused by growth
• Does not involve any increase in size
• Happens due to changes in turgor pressure (water in cells)
• Example: Mimosa pudica (touch-me-not) folding leaves when touched — no growth involved
• These are nastic movements
