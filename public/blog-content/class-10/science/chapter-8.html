Chapter Overview

Life's Drive to Continue Itself

Reproduction is not essential for the survival of an individual — but it is absolutely essential for the survival of a species. This chapter covers two major strategies: asexual reproduction (one parent, exact clones) and sexual reproduction (two parents, genetic variation).

We explore reproduction in plants (vegetative propagation, flower structure, pollination), in simple organisms (Amoeba, Hydra, Planaria), and in humans — from puberty through embryo development to birth.

01Types of Reproduction
1
Fundamentals

Sexual vs Asexual Reproduction

Reproduction is the biological process by which organisms produce offspring. Information is passed from parent to offspring through DNA — the genetic material in chromosomes. There are two fundamentally different strategies:

Two Parents
Sexual Reproduction
Requires fusion of male and female gametes. Offspring are genetically unique — not identical to either parent.
Two parents required — male and female
Involves fusion of male and female gametes (fertilisation)
More variation in offspring — allows natural selection
Slower; fewer offspring per cycle
Examples: Humans, flowering plants, most animals
One Parent
Asexual Reproduction
No fusion of gametes. Offspring are exact genetic copies (clones) of the parent. Only one parent involved.
Only one parent required
Does NOT involve fusion of gametes
Offspring are identical clones of parent
Faster; produces large numbers quickly
Examples: Amoeba, Hydra, Yeast, Planaria, Spirogyra
🧬 DNA & Variation: DNA copying is never 100% perfect — small errors (mutations) create variation. Sexual reproduction adds more variation by combining DNA from two parents. Variation is the raw material for evolution.
02Asexual Reproduction
2
One-Parent Strategies

Modes of Asexual Reproduction

Asexual reproduction uses several mechanisms — all requiring only one parent and producing genetically identical offspring:

Mode 1
Fission
Amoeba · Plasmodium · Bacteria
Binary fission: Parent cell divides into 2 equal daughter cells. Example: Amoeba, Bacteria
Multiple fission: Single cell divides simultaneously into many daughter cells. Example: Plasmodium (malarial parasite), Amoeba (during unfavourable conditions)
Each daughter cell is an identical copy of the parent
Mode 2
Budding
Hydra · Yeast
New individual grows as a protrusion (bud) on the parent body
Bud grows, matures and eventually separates to become an independent organism
Hydra — small bud emerges from body wall, grows, detaches
Yeast reproduces by budding under suitable nutrient conditions
Mode 3
Regeneration
Planaria · Hydra · Starfish
Ability to regrow lost or injured body parts into complete organisms
Planaria — if cut into pieces, each piece regenerates into a complete new organism
Specialised cells (called neoblasts in Planaria) rapidly proliferate to replace missing parts
Not all organisms can regenerate — only those with highly undifferentiated cells
Mode 4
Fragmentation
Lichens · Spirogyra · Algae
Parent body breaks into fragments — each fragment grows into a new organism
Spirogyra — filamentous alga breaks into pieces, each piece grows independently
Lichens — reproduce by fragmentation under harsh conditions
Different from regeneration — fragmentation is a reproductive strategy, not repair

Flowering Plants

Flower Structure & Pollination

In angiosperms (flowering plants), the flower is the reproductive organ. It contains both male and female parts and facilitates sexual reproduction through pollination and fertilisation.

🌸 Structure of a Flower — Parts & Labels
Outermost Whorl
Sepals (Calyx)
Green in colour. Protect the flower bud before it opens. Collectively called calyx.
2nd Whorl — Attract Pollinators
Petals (Corolla)
Colourful structures that attract bees, butterflies, birds and other pollinators. Collectively called corolla.
Male Reproductive Part
Stamen (Androecium)
Consists of anther (produces pollen = male gametes) and filament (stalk supporting anther). Pollen released when anther bursts.
Female Reproductive Part
Carpel / Pistil (Gynoecium)
Consists of stigma (receives pollen), style (connects stigma to ovary), and ovary (contains ovules with female gametes).
🌺 Bisexual flowers have both stamens and carpels (e.g., Hibiscus, Rose). Unisexual flowers have either stamens OR carpels only (e.g., Corn, Papaya). Each ovule contains one egg cell (female gamete).

✂️ Stem Cutting — Vegetative Propagation
Stem Cutting
Short lengths of stem cut at a slant with a blade. Planted in soil (sand + compost). Adventitious roots develop from the cut end. Examples: Rose, Coleus, Begonia.
Root Cutting
Root sections cut and placed in soil. Develop shoots and roots to become independent plants. Examples: Lime, Tamarind.
Tissue Culture (Micropropagation)
Meristematic tissue grown in sterile nutrient medium → callus → new plants. Produces disease-free, large numbers of plants. Used for orchids, bananas, potato.

05Human Reproduction

Reproductive Systems

Male & Female Reproductive Systems

Puberty is the period when a young individual develops into a sexually mature adult — essential before reproduction can occur. The human reproductive systems are designed for the fusion of gametes and development of the embryo.

♂ Male Reproductive System — Anatomy
♀ Female Reproductive System — Anatomy
Male System
♂ Male Reproductive Organs
Testes (pair) — produce sperms (male gametes) and testosterone hormone. Located in scrotum (outside body — lower temperature needed for sperm production)
Vas deferens — muscular tube that carries sperms from testes toward urethra
Seminal vesicles — secrete fluid that nourishes sperm; forms part of semen
Prostate gland — secretes fluid that activates sperm motility
Urethra — common passage for urine and semen (not simultaneously)
Sperms = male gametes with head (nucleus), middle piece (mitochondria), and tail (flagellum for movement)
Female System
♀ Female Reproductive Organs
Ovaries (pair) — contain thousands of eggs (ova); secrete estrogen and progesterone
Fallopian tubes (oviducts) — carry egg from ovary to uterus; site of fertilisation
Uterus (womb) — pear-shaped muscular organ; embryo implants and develops here
Endometrium — inner lining of uterus; thickens to receive embryo; shed during menstruation if no fertilisation
Cervix — lower narrow part of uterus; opening into vagina
Vagina — muscular canal; sperms enter here; also birth canal
🩸 Menstruation: If the egg released each month is NOT fertilised, the uterus lining (endometrium) breaks down and is shed as blood and mucus through the vagina. Lasts 2–8 days; cycle repeats approximately every 28 days.

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is reproduction? Why is it important?
Reproduction is the biological process by which a living organism produces offspring similar to itself.

It is not essential for individual survival, but it is essential for the perpetuation (continuation) of the species. Without reproduction, species would become extinct.

Information is transferred from parents to offspring in the form of DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid) — the genetic material found in chromosomes in the nucleus of each cell. Reproduction ensures this genetic information is passed on to the next generation.
Q2. What are the two types of reproduction? How do they differ?
Sexual Reproduction:
• Requires two parents (male and female)
• Involves fusion of male and female gametes (fertilisation)
• Offspring have genetic variation — not identical to parents
• Slower process; fewer offspring
• Examples: Humans, flowering plants

Asexual Reproduction:
• Requires only one parent
• Does NOT involve fusion of gametes
• Offspring are exact genetic copies (clones) of the parent
• Faster; produces large numbers
• Examples: Amoeba (fission), Hydra (budding), Planaria (regeneration)
Q3. Describe the structure of a flower and its reproductive parts.
A flower has four main parts (whorls):

1. Sepals — green, outermost; protect the flower bud. Collectively: calyx
2. Petals — colourful; attract pollinators. Collectively: corolla
3. Stamens — male reproductive organs; consist of anther (produces pollen/male gametes) and filament (stalk). Collectively: androecium
4. Carpels/Pistil — female reproductive organs; consist of stigma (receives pollen), style (tube), and ovary (contains ovules/eggs). Collectively: gynoecium

Bisexual flowers: both stamens and carpels present (e.g., Hibiscus)
Unisexual flowers: only stamens OR only carpels (e.g., Corn)
Q4. What is pollination? What is fertilisation? What happens after fertilisation?
Pollination: Transfer of pollen grains from the anther of one flower to the stigma of the same or a different flower.

Fertilisation: Fusion of the male gamete (pollen) with the female gamete (egg/ovum) inside the ovule. Occurs after the pollen tube grows down through the style.

After fertilisation:
• Zygote → develops into embryo
• Ovule → develops into seed
• Ovary → develops into fruit

The seed contains the embryo (future plant) and food reserve. The fruit protects the seed and aids dispersal.
Q5. Describe the male and female reproductive systems in humans.
Male reproductive system:
• Testes (pair) — produce sperms (male gametes) and testosterone hormone
• Vas deferens — carries sperms from testes
• Prostate gland + Seminal vesicles — secrete fluids that form semen
• Penis/Urethra — semen exits through here

Female reproductive system:
• Ovaries (pair) — contain thousands of eggs (female gametes); secrete estrogen and progesterone
• Oviducts/Fallopian tubes — carries egg from ovary to uterus; site of fertilisation
• Uterus — embryo implants and develops here during pregnancy
• Vagina — sperms enter through here

Fertilisation occurs in the fallopian tube.
Q6. What is menstruation? What is the menstrual cycle?
Every month, one egg is released from an ovary (ovulation). The uterus lining (endometrium) thickens in preparation for a potential pregnancy.

If the egg is NOT fertilised:
The uterus lining breaks down and is shed along with blood and mucus through the vagina. This is called menstruation. It typically lasts 2 to 8 days and recurs approximately every 28 days — forming the menstrual cycle.

If the egg IS fertilised:
The zygote implants in the uterus wall and menstruation does not occur. Pregnancy begins.
Q7. Describe the development of an embryo from fertilisation to birth.
1. Fertilisation — sperm + egg → zygote (in fallopian tube)
2. Cleavage — zygote repeatedly divides → ball of cells
3. Implantation — ball of cells attaches to uterus wall
4. Embryo — cells differentiate into tissues and organs
5. Foetus — embryo stage showing recognisable features of the mature organism (hands, legs, face)
6. Gestation — foetus develops for nine months inside the mother's womb
7. Birth — fully developed baby delivered

The placenta connects the embryo to the uterine wall, supplying nutrients and oxygen from the mother's blood.
Q8. What is vegetative propagation? What are its advantages?
Vegetative propagation is a form of asexual reproduction in plants where new plants develop from vegetative parts (roots, stems, leaves) rather than seeds.

Natural examples:
• Potato — buds (eyes) on stem develop into plants
• Bryophyllum — leaf margin buds grow into plants
• Sweet potato / Dahlia — roots detach and give rise to new plants

Advantages:
1. Only method for seedless plants (sugarcane, banana, rose)
2. Produces exact copies of parent plant
3. Large numbers of plants produced quickly
4. Disease-free plants can be propagated
5. Plants bear fruit/flowers faster than seed-grown plants
Q9. Explain the artificial methods of vegetative propagation.
1. Cutting:
• Stem cutting — short stem lengths planted in soil → develop adventitious roots. Examples: Rose, Coleus, Begonia
• Root cutting — root pieces develop into plants. Examples: Lime, Tamarind

2. Layering:
• Mound layering — branch bent to touch soil; covered with soil; roots develop at node; then cut and planted
• Air layering — part of stem bark removed, wrapped in moist cotton in a bag; roots develop; then cut off. Example: Rubber

3. Grafting:
• Root portion used = stock; stem portion used = scion
• Bud grafting — dormant bud grafted onto stock (e.g., Rose)
• Stem grafting — scion inserted into cut stock (e.g., Mango, Apple, Citrus)

4. Tissue culture (Micropropagation):
Meristematic tissue grown in nutrient medium → callus → propagated into new plants. Used for orchids, bananas, disease-free plants.
Q10. What is hybridisation? What are its advantages?
Hybridisation is the mating of two individuals of different genotypes to produce a hybrid with desirable characteristics.

Process:
1. Select male and female parent plants
2. Emasculation — remove anthers from female flower
3. Bagging — cover emasculated flower to prevent unwanted cross-pollination
4. Transfer pollen from male parent to stigma of female parent

Advantages:
1. Higher yielding, better quality seeds
2. Disease and drought resistant varieties
3. Adaptation to wider range of habitats
4. Insect and pest resistance

Examples: hybrid rice, hybrid wheat, hybrid maize
Q11. Describe the different modes of asexual reproduction.
1. Fission — parent cell splits into two or more
• Binary fission: single cell divides into 2 equal daughters (e.g., Amoeba, Bacteria)
• Multiple fission: single cell divides into many simultaneously (e.g., Plasmodium, Amoeba)

2. Budding — new individual grows as a protrusion (bud) from parent; separates and becomes independent. Example: Hydra, Yeast

3. Regeneration — body parts regenerate into whole organisms. Example: Planaria (cut into pieces → each piece grows into complete organism), Hydra

4. Fragmentation — organism breaks into fragments; each fragment develops into new organism. Example: Lichens, Spirogyra

All these produce genetically identical offspring (clones) and require only one parent.
Q12. What are sexually transmitted diseases (STDs)? What are contraceptive methods?
STDs are infections transferred through sexual contact:
• HIV/AIDS — most serious; weakens immune system; no cure
• Syphilis — bacterial; treatable with antibiotics
• Gonorrhea — bacterial; treatable
• Herpes — viral; causes sores
Condoms provide significant protection against STDs.

Contraceptive methods:
• Natural — abstinence (avoiding sexual contact)
• Barrier — condoms (male/female); physically prevent sperm from reaching egg
• Oral contraceptives — hormonal pills; prevent ovulation
• Implants — copper-T or loop inserted in uterus; prevents implantation
• Surgical — Vasectomy (males: vas deferens cut); Tubectomy (females: fallopian tube cut/tied)
