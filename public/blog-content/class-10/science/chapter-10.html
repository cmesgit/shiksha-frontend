Chapter Overview

The Science of Light & Optics

Light is a form of energy that enables us to see objects. It travels from one point to another along a straight line — a property called rectilinear propagation. This chapter explores two key optical phenomena: reflection (light bouncing off surfaces) and refraction (light bending as it crosses media).

We study how spherical mirrors — concave and convex — form images at different positions, how glass bends light according to Snell's Law, and how lenses focus light to create real and virtual images used in everything from eyeglasses to cameras.

01Reflection of Light
1
Bouncing of Light

Laws of Reflection & Plane Mirrors

When light falls on a highly polished surface such as a mirror, most of it gets reflected back. Reflection follows two fundamental laws that hold for all types of mirrors — plane, concave, and convex.

🔦
Fundamental Principles
The Two Laws of Reflection
01
Equal Angles
The angle of incidence is always equal to the angle of reflection.
∠i = ∠r
02
Coplanar Rays
The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.

Image formed by a Plane Mirror — when an object is placed in front of a plane mirror, the image has these properties:

👁️
Virtual & Erect
The image is virtual (cannot be obtained on a screen) and erect (upright — same orientation as the object).
↔️
Laterally Inverted
Left side of the object appears as the right side of the image, and vice versa.
📏
Same Size
The size of the image is exactly equal to the size of the object. Magnification = 1.
📐
Equal Distance
The image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it. Object distance = Image distance.

Curved Reflecting Surfaces

Concave & Convex Mirrors

Spherical mirrors are mirrors whose reflecting surface is a part of a hollow sphere. Think of the curved surface of a shiny spoon — if it curves inward, it acts as a concave mirror; if outward, a convex mirror.

Reflects Inward
Concave Mirror
Also called: Converging Mirror
Converges parallel rays to a real focus point in front of the mirror
Focal length (f) is negative by sign convention
Can form both real and virtual images depending on object position
Used in torches, solar furnaces, shaving mirrors, dentist mirrors
Reflects Outward
Convex Mirror
Also called: Diverging Mirror
Diverges parallel rays — appears to come from a virtual focus behind the mirror
Focal length (f) is positive by sign convention
Always forms a virtual, erect, and diminished image
Used as rear-view mirrors — gives wide field of view

Key Terms related to spherical mirrors:

P
Pole
Geometric central point of the reflecting surface. All distances measured from here.
C
Centre of Curvature
Centre of the sphere of which the mirror forms a part. Distance PC = R.
F
Focus Point
Point where all parallel rays meet after reflection. Distance PF = f.
f = R/2
R
Radius of Curvature
Separation between pole and centre of curvature. R = 2f always.
R = 2f
PA
Principal Axis
Imaginary straight line joining the pole and the centre of curvature.
A
Aperture
The effective width (diameter) of the circular reflecting surface of the mirror.

Uses of Concave Mirror
Converging mirror applications
1Used in torches, search lights, and vehicle headlights to produce powerful parallel beams
2Used as shaving mirror to see a large, magnified, virtual image of the face
3Used by dentists to see enlarged images of teeth

Diverging mirror applications

04Refraction of Light
4
Bending of Light

Refraction, Snell's Law & Refractive Index

Refraction is the bending of light as it travels from one transparent medium to another. It occurs because the speed of light changes when it enters a different medium. Light slows down when entering a denser medium, causing it to bend towards the normal; it speeds up in a rarer medium, bending away from the normal.

Rarer → Denser Medium
Air to Glass (Bends Towards Normal)
Speed decreases as light enters denser medium
Refracted ray bends towards the normal
Angle of refraction (r) < angle of incidence (i)
Example: Light entering glass/water from air
Denser → Rarer Medium
Glass to Air (Bends Away from Normal)
Speed increases as light enters rarer medium
Refracted ray bends away from the normal
Angle of refraction (r) > angle of incidence (i)
Example: Light exiting glass/water into air

Everyday phenomena due to refraction:

🪨
Raised Stone in Water
A stone at the bottom of a water tub appears to be raised (closer to the surface) because light bends away from normal when exiting water.
🐟
Fish Looks Bigger
A fish in an aquarium appears larger than its actual size because refraction of light at the water–air interface magnifies the image.
✏️
Pencil in Water
A pencil partially immersed in water appears displaced/bent at the interface of air and water — a classic refraction effect.
🔲 Refraction through a Rectangular Glass Slab: When a ray passes through a glass slab, it bends towards the normal at entry (air → glass) and away at exit (glass → air). The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced. If the ray is incident normally (0°), it passes straight through without bending.
Snell's Law of Refraction & Refractive Index
sin i / sin r = n₂/n₁ = n₂₁
— Snell's Law —
For a given colour & pair of media, this ratio is always constant
Refractive Indices

Laws of Refraction: (1) The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal to the interface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane. (2) The ratio of sin(i) to sin(r) is a constant for a given pair of media — Snell's Law.

05Spherical Lenses
5
Transparent Curved Glass

Convex & Concave Lenses

A lens is a transparent material (usually glass) bound by two surfaces, of which one or both are spherical. Unlike mirrors that reflect, lenses refract light. There are two main types based on the curvature of their surfaces.

Bulges Outward
Convex Lens
Also called: Converging Lens
Converges a beam of parallel light rays to a real focus point (F₂) on the other side
Focal length (f) is always positive
Can form both real and virtual images depending on object position
Used in magnifying glasses, cameras, microscopes, human eye
Curves Inward
Concave Lens
Also called: Diverging Lens
Diverges a beam of parallel light rays — rays appear to come from focus F₁ on the same side
Focal length (f) is always negative
Always forms a virtual, erect, and diminished image
Used in correcting short-sightedness (myopia)

Key Terms for Spherical Lenses:

O
Optical Centre
Central point of the lens. A ray passing through O goes straight without any deviation.
F
Principal Focus
Point where parallel rays converge (convex) or appear to diverge from (concave) after refraction.
2F
Centre of Curvature
Centre of the sphere of which each lens surface forms a part. Also called 2F₁ and 2F₂.
f
Focal Length
Distance between optical centre O and the principal focus F. Positive for convex, negative for concave.
PA
Principal Axis
Imaginary straight line passing through the two centres of curvature of the lens.
A
Aperture
Effective diameter of the circular outline of a spherical lens — determines light-gathering ability.

🔍 Concave Lens — Image Formation: For any object position (from infinity to the optical centre), a concave lens always forms an image between F₁ and O on the same side as the object. The image is always virtual, erect, and diminished.

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What are the two laws of reflection?
Law 1: The angle of incidence is always equal to the angle of reflection (∠i = ∠r). Law 2: The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane. These laws apply to both plane and spherical mirrors.
Q2. What is the difference between a concave mirror and a convex mirror?
Concave mirror — reflecting surface curves inward; also called a converging mirror because it converges parallel rays to a real focus point in front of the mirror. Used in torches, solar furnaces, dentist's mirrors. Convex mirror — reflecting surface curves outward; also called a diverging mirror. It always forms a virtual, erect, diminished image. Used as rear-view mirrors in vehicles because it provides a wide field of view.
Q3. What is the relationship between focal length (f) and radius of curvature (R)?
The focal length is exactly half the radius of curvature: f = R/2. This means the focus point F lies midway between the pole P and the centre of curvature C. For a concave mirror, both R and f are negative (by sign convention). For a convex mirror, both are positive.
Q4. State the mirror formula and explain each term.
Mirror formula: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u, where f = focal length (distance from pole to focus), v = image distance (from pole), u = object distance (from pole). All distances are measured from the pole P. Distances in the direction of incident light are positive; against incident light are negative (New Cartesian sign convention). The relationship R = 2f also holds: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u.
Q5. What is magnification in a spherical mirror? What do positive and negative values mean?
Magnification (m) = height of image / height of object = −v/u. If m > 1: image is enlarged (magnified). If m = 1: image is the same size. If m < 1: image is diminished. A negative m means the image is real and inverted. A positive m means the image is virtual and erect.
Q6. What is refraction of light? Why does it occur?
Refraction is the bending of light when it passes from one transparent medium to another. It occurs because the speed of light changes as it enters a different medium. When light travels from a rarer medium (like air) to a denser medium (like glass), it bends towards the normal because its speed decreases. When it travels from denser to rarer, it bends away from the normal because its speed increases.
Q7. State Snell's Law of refraction.
Snell's Law: The ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant for a given pair of media and given colour of light. Mathematically: sin i / sin r = n₂₁ = n₂/n₁. This constant is the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first. It is also equal to the ratio of the speed of light in the first medium to the speed in the second medium.
Q8. What is the refractive index and what are its values for common materials?
Refractive index (n) = speed of light in vacuum (c) / speed of light in medium (v). It has no units. Common values: Air ≈ 1.0003 (approximately 1), Water (nw) = 1.33, Glass (ng) = 1.52. A higher refractive index means light travels slower in that medium. The speed of light in vacuum is c = 3×10⁸ m/s.
Q9. What happens when a ray of light passes through a rectangular glass slab?
When a light ray passes through a rectangular glass slab: (1) At the first surface (air to glass), the ray bends towards the normal. (2) At the second surface (glass to air), the ray bends away from the normal. The emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced (shifted sideways). If the ray is incident normally (90° to the surface), it passes straight through without any bending.
Q10. What is the lens formula and how is it different from the mirror formula?
Lens formula: 1/f = 1/v − 1/u. The key difference from the mirror formula (1/f = 1/v + 1/u) is the minus sign in the lens formula. All distances for lenses are measured from the optical centre O. For a convex lens, f is positive. For a concave lens, f is negative. This is opposite to mirrors where concave → negative f and convex → positive f.
Q11. What is power of a lens? What is its SI unit?
Power of a lens (P) is defined as the reciprocal of its focal length: P = 1/f (when f is in metres). It measures the degree of convergence or divergence of light. SI unit: dioptre (D). 1 dioptre = power of a lens with focal length of 1 metre. Convex lens (converging) has positive power. Concave lens (diverging) has negative power. When lenses are in contact: Pnet = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …
Q12. What is the nature of image formed by a concave mirror when the object is placed between F and P?
When an object is placed between the focus F and the pole P of a concave mirror, this is a special case. The image formed is: Virtual (cannot be obtained on a screen), Erect (same orientation as object), and Enlarged (magnified). The image is formed behind the mirror. This is why concave mirrors are used as shaving mirrors and by dentists — the face/teeth placed close to the mirror gives a magnified, erect, virtual image.
