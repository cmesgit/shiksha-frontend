Chapter Overview

Using What the Earth Gives Us — Wisely

Nature gives us soil, air, water, forests, wildlife, coal and petroleum — everything we need to survive. But these resources are finite, and humans have consumed them faster than the planet can renew them. Management of natural resources is the practice of using these gifts in a way that doesn't exhaust them or damage the ecosystem that produced them.

This chapter explores how India manages its natural wealth — through laws and Acts, grassroots movements like Chipko Andolan, multi-crore projects like the Ganga Action Plan, everyday practices like the 3Rs, and ancient water-harvesting systems like Khadins, Kulhs and Bawlis. Conservation is not a single law or machine — it's a way of thinking.

Cleaning a Holy River

The Ganga Action Plan & Conservation Movements

Nature gives us the basic resources — soil, air, water, forests, wildlife, coal and petroleum. To protect these, governments and citizens have created national and international laws and Acts. One of India's most famous projects is the Ganga Action Plan, a multi-crore project launched in 1985 to improve the quality of the river Ganga.

A 1993–1994 survey measured total coliform — a group of bacteria found in the human intestine, used as an indicator of sewage contamination. Measurements were in MPN/100 ml (Most Probable Number per 100 ml of water). The results revealed just how badly polluted the river had become:

Even at Rishikesh — where the river emerges pristine from the Himalayas — coliform was already above the desired level. By Kannauj, it had tripled. This data launched the first scientific clean-up of the Ganga.
Amrita Devi Bishnoi
18th Century · Rajasthan
National Award for Wildlife Conservation
The Amrita Devi Bishnoi National Award for Wildlife Conservation honours the memory of Amrita Devi, who along with 363 other villagers of the Bishnoi community lost her life in 1730 while protecting Khejri trees from being cut in Rajasthan. The villagers hugged the trees and refused to let them fall — the earliest recorded Chipko-like movement, and a founding moment of Indian environmentalism.
Chipko Andolan
Early 1970s · Garhwal
Grassroot Forest Movement
Chipko (literally "to hug") originated in Garhwal in the early 1970s as a grassroot effort to end the alienation of people from their forest. Villagers — mostly women — would hug trees to prevent contractors from felling them. In 1972, a parallel movement protected the Sal forests of West Bengal. These movements established a powerful principle: those who depend on forests should have a say in managing them.

02The Three R's to Save the Environment
2
A Simple Rule, Huge Impact

Reduce, Reuse & Recycle

The 3 Rs are the simplest framework for conserving natural resources. They aren't in random order — Reduce first, Reuse second, Recycle last, because each step consumes a little more energy than the previous one. Always prefer the higher rung when you can.

Reduce
Use less of a resource in the first place. Save what you would have used — turn off unused lights and fans, take shorter showers, buy only what you need. The cleanest resource is the one never used.
Save the resource
Reuse
Use the same item again and again for the same or a different purpose. Refill water bottles, turn jars into storage, pass on clothes. Reuse is better than recycling because it saves energy.
Use it again
Recycle
Segregate waste (paper, plastic, glass, metal) so it can be reprocessed into new products. Recycling saves raw materials but still consumes energy during processing — so it ranks third.
Turn waste into things
Why this order matters: Reducing uses no energy (you just don't do something). Reusing uses a tiny amount (washing a jar). Recycling uses a lot — collection, transportation, cleaning, melting, reshaping. So every time you reach for an R, try the top one first.

Who Has a Stake in the Forest?

Forest Conservation & Stakeholders

Forests are biodiversity hotspots — areas packed with bacteria, fungi, flowering plants, insects, birds, and mammals. The biodiversity of a region is simply the number of species of different life forms found there. Loss of biodiversity leads to ecological imbalance — one lost species weakens the whole web.

A stakeholder is anyone with an interest or concern in a resource. Successful forest management means balancing the needs of four different groups, each of whom sees the forest through a different lens:

Sustainable management means using forest resources wisely so that enough remains for future generations — and the interests of all four stakeholder groups are balanced against each other.
01
Local People
Communities living in or near the forest who depend on it for firewood, food, fodder, and daily livelihood.
02
Forest Department
Government body that owns forest land and controls access to its resources through laws and permits.
03
Industrialists
Businesses that use forest products — timber, resin, bamboo, herbs, tendu leaves — as raw materials for production.

Wildlife Enthusiasts
Conservationists and nature lovers who want to preserve forests and wildlife for their ecological and intrinsic value.
04Water as a Resource — Dams & Harvesting

The Most Basic Resource

Water, Dams & Ancient Harvesting

Water is the basic necessity for all terrestrial forms of life. Regions of water scarcity closely correlate with regions of acute poverty — water poverty and economic poverty travel together. Failure to sustain water availability has resulted from loss of vegetation cover, diversion for high water-demanding crops, pollution from industries and urban waste, and less rainfall.

For centuries, Indians used dams, tanks, and canals to manage water. Today's large dams are descendants of those traditions — but modern scale brings modern problems.

Advantages of Dams
1
Ensure adequate water for irrigation, especially in dry seasons.
2
Generate hydroelectricity — clean, renewable power.
3
Provide continuous supply of water to towns and villages year-round.
Disadvantages
1
No equitable distribution — farmers near the dam get plenty; those far away get little.
2
Large numbers of people are displaced without compensation.
3
Involves huge amounts of public money without proportionate benefits.
4
Causes deforestation and loss of biological diversity from submergence.

Water Harvesting — an ancient Indian art

The aim of water harvesting is to develop primary resources of land and water, and to produce secondary resources of plants and animals — in a way that doesn't cause ecological imbalance. India has a rich heritage of locale-specific harvesting systems, each designed for its own terrain and climate, preventing mismanagement and over-exploitation.

The Resources Running Out

Coal & Petroleum — Use Them Wisely

Coal and petroleum are generally called fossil fuels. They were formed from the disintegration of biomass over millions of years. No matter how carefully we use them, they will get exhausted in the future. Both fuels also contain hydrogen, nitrogen and sulphur — which turn into pollutants when burnt.

Petroleum, once the star of industrial civilization, is now projected to run out within a human lifetime at current consumption rates. Coal lasts five times longer — but burning it is the biggest single driver of climate change.

Why use fossil fuels judiciously?

Use public transport instead of private vehicles — cuts petrol use and CO₂ drastically.
Use CFL or LED bulbs — they use a fraction of the electricity of incandescents.
Take the stairs instead of the lift wherever you can — saves a surprising amount of power.
Switch off lights, fans, and appliances when not in use. Unplug standby chargers.
The real point: Fossil fuels are not just finite — they're also polluting. Every litre of petrol and every kilogram of coal contributes to the greenhouse effect, acid rain, and smog. Using them wisely buys humanity time to shift to renewables.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Why is it necessary to conserve our environment?
The environment provides us with all the natural resources — soil, air, water, forests, minerals, wildlife, coal and petroleum — that humans depend on for survival. Conservation ensures these resources remain available for future generations, maintains ecological balance, protects biodiversity, and prevents long-term damage like climate change, pollution, and loss of species. Without conservation, short-term gains would destroy the very systems that sustain life on Earth.
Q2. Define sustainable development.
Sustainable development is the use of resources wisely to meet the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It balances three goals: economic growth, environmental protection, and social equity. In the context of natural resources, it means managing forests, water, coal, and petroleum in ways that don't exhaust them or damage the ecosystem.
Q3. Name any two exhaustible resources.
Two exhaustible (non-renewable) resources are coal and petroleum. Both are fossil fuels formed from the disintegration of biomass over millions of years. At current rates of consumption, petroleum is expected to last around 40 years and coal around 200 years — hence the urgency to use them judiciously and develop renewable alternatives.
Q4. What is the most conducive pH range for the life of fresh water plants?
The most conducive pH range for fresh water plants and aquatic life is pH 6.5 to 7.5 — slightly acidic to neutral. Significant deviation from this range — caused by industrial effluents, acid rain, or agricultural runoff — disrupts aquatic ecosystems and can kill fish, plants and microorganisms. pH is one of the key indicators used to assess water pollution.
Q5. List two advantages of water harvesting.
(i) Recharges groundwater: Water harvesting structures help replenish underground aquifers, raising the water table and ensuring supply in dry months.

(ii) Prevents wastage and ecological imbalance: Captured water doesn't run off or evaporate uselessly. In systems like Khadin, the water also provides moisture for vegetation, avoids breeding grounds for mosquitoes, and protects groundwater from human and animal waste.
Q6. Why is reuse better than recycle?
Reuse is better than recycle because it saves energy. When we reuse an item (like a glass jar or plastic container) we simply wash and use it again — no energy is spent on processing. Recycling, on the other hand, requires collection, transportation, cleaning, melting/shredding, and reshaping — all of which consume significant energy and can release pollutants. Reuse also keeps items in circulation longer before they eventually enter the recycling stream.
Q7. What are Khadins? Where are they found?
Khadins are an ancient water-harvesting system found in the arid Jaisalmer region of Rajasthan. They consist of an earthen embankment (bund) built across the lower hill slopes to capture rainwater runoff. The water collects behind the bund and slowly seeps into the soil, recharging wells and providing moisture for cultivation.

Advantages: water doesn't evaporate (seeps underground), recharges wells, provides soil moisture for vegetation, doesn't breed mosquitoes, and protects groundwater from contamination.
Q8. List two steps you would take to conserve electricity in your house.
(i) Replace incandescent bulbs with LEDs or CFLs — these use up to 80% less electricity for the same light output.

(ii) Switch off lights, fans, and appliances when not in use. Unplug chargers and turn off standby power on TVs and computers.

Additional measures: use BEE 5-star rated appliances, take the stairs instead of the lift, use natural daylight, and set air conditioners at 24–26°C instead of lower temperatures.
Q9. Who are called stakeholders?
A stakeholder is any person or group that has an interest or concern in a resource and its management. In the context of forests, there are four main stakeholders:

(i) Local people who live in or near the forest and depend on it for firewood, food, and livelihood;
(ii) The Forest Department of the Government, which owns and controls forest land;
(iii) Industrialists who use forest products (wood, resin, bamboo, etc.);
(iv) Wildlife & nature enthusiasts / conservationists who want to preserve nature.

Successful forest management requires balancing the needs of all four groups.
Q10. Name some traditional water harvesting systems in India.
India has a rich heritage of locale-specific water harvesting systems:

Khadin, tanks, nadis — Rajasthan
Bandharas, tals — Maharashtra
Bundhis — Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh
Pynes, ahars — Bihar

These techniques were developed over centuries and are locale-specific, designed to prevent mismanagement and over-exploitation.
Q11. How is mining a cause of pollution?
Mining causes several kinds of pollution:

(i) Land degradation: Removes vegetation and topsoil, leaves behind huge pits and spoil heaps that cannot easily be reclaimed.
(ii) Air pollution: Dust particles from blasting and crushing, plus toxic gases from processing.
(iii) Water pollution: Acid mine drainage, heavy metals (arsenic, mercury, lead) leach into rivers and groundwater.
(iv) Noise pollution and disruption of wildlife habitats.
(v) Solid waste: Slag and tailings pile up as long-term hazards.
Q12. Make a list of four forest products that we use.
Four commonly used forest products are:

(i) Timber / wood — for construction, furniture, paper.
(ii) Firewood & charcoal — fuel for cooking and heating.
(iii) Resins, gums & latex — raw materials for industries (turpentine, rubber, varnish).
(iv) Medicinal plants & herbs — source of Ayurvedic and modern medicines. Others include bamboo, cane, honey, tendu leaves (bidi), and fodder.
Q13. How is burning of fossil fuels affecting our environment?
Burning fossil fuels releases several harmful gases:

(i) Carbon dioxide (CO₂): A greenhouse gas that traps heat in the atmosphere, causing global warming and climate change.
(ii) Sulphur oxides (SO₂) and Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ): Acidic in nature, they dissolve in rainwater to produce acid rain, which damages buildings, soil, forests, and aquatic life.
(iii) Carbon monoxide (CO): Highly toxic, causes respiratory problems.
(iv) Particulate matter & soot: Causes air pollution, respiratory diseases, and smog in cities.
Q14. Suggest two measures for controlling CO₂ levels in the atmosphere.
(i) Afforestation and reforestation — planting trees on a large scale. Trees absorb CO₂ during photosynthesis and act as carbon sinks. Protecting existing forests from being cut is equally important.

(ii) Shift to renewable energy — replace fossil fuel-based electricity with solar, wind, hydro, and nuclear power, which emit little to no CO₂. Improving energy efficiency of buildings and vehicles, using public transport, and adopting electric vehicles also reduce CO₂ output.
Q15. Why should we conserve forest and wildlife?
Forests and wildlife deserve conservation because:

(i) Biodiversity: Forests are hotspots of biodiversity — home to countless species of plants, animals, fungi, and microorganisms. Loss of any species disturbs the ecological balance.
(ii) Ecological services: Forests regulate climate, produce oxygen, absorb CO₂, prevent soil erosion, maintain the water cycle, and support pollination.
(iii) Resources: Forests provide timber, medicinal plants, food, fodder, and raw materials for industries.
(iv) Cultural & livelihood value: Many indigenous communities depend on forests for their way of life.
Q16. What are three main problems from dams?
(i) Social displacement: Large numbers of people — often tribal and farming communities — are forcibly displaced from their homes and land, frequently without adequate compensation or rehabilitation.

(ii) Environmental damage: Construction of big dams causes deforestation, submergence of large forest areas, loss of biological diversity, and disruption of river ecosystems. Fish migration is blocked and downstream flow is altered.

(iii) Economic waste: Huge amounts of public money are invested, but proportionate benefits often don't reach the people. Water is not distributed equitably — those near the dam get plenty, while farmers far away remain without.
Q17. How can you reduce energy consumption at your level?
At a personal level, you can significantly reduce energy consumption through:

(i) Use CFL or LED bulbs instead of incandescent ones — they use 1/4th the energy for the same light.
(ii) Switch off appliances when not in use — lights, fans, TV, geyser, chargers. Avoid standby mode.
(iii) Use public transport, cycle, or walk instead of private vehicles wherever possible — saves petrol and reduces CO₂.

Additional: take stairs instead of the lift, use solar water heaters, air-dry clothes instead of using the dryer, and choose BEE 5-star rated appliances.
Q18. Explain the meaning of Reduce, Recycle and Reuse in your own words.
Reduce: Use less of a resource in the first place — turn off the tap while brushing, take shorter showers, switch off unused lights and fans, avoid buying things you don't need. This is the most powerful of the three.

Reuse: Use the same item again and again for the same or different purposes before throwing it away — refill water bottles, use jam jars as storage containers, pass down clothes. Reuse saves energy because nothing needs reprocessing.

Recycle: When something truly can't be reused, segregate the waste (paper, plastic, glass, metal) so it can be reprocessed into new products. Recycling saves raw materials but consumes energy during processing — which is why it ranks third.
Q19. What is the main objective of water harvesting techniques? Name 3 ancient water harvesting structures used in India. Mention 3 causes for failure to sustain water availability underground.
Main objective: To develop primary resources of land and water and produce secondary resources of plants and animals in a manner that does not cause ecological imbalance. The goal is to capture rainwater where it falls, recharge groundwater, and ensure year-round availability for agriculture and drinking.

Three ancient structures: Khadin (Rajasthan), Kulhs (Himachal Pradesh), Bawlis / Stepwells (Delhi region). Others include Pynes in Bihar, Eris in Tamil Nadu, and Bandharas in Maharashtra.

Three causes of failure to sustain groundwater:
(i) Loss of vegetation cover — removes the sponge that allows rainfall to seep in.
(ii) Diversion for high water-demanding crops (like sugarcane, rice in dry areas) exceeds natural recharge.
(iii) Industrial and urban pollution plus less rainfall due to climate change further deplete groundwater.
Q20. Discuss the damage caused to forests by (a) building rest houses for tourists, (b) grazing domestic animals, (c) tourists throwing litter in national parks.
(a) Building rest houses: Requires clearing trees, disturbs wildlife habitats, introduces noise and light pollution, and fragments continuous forest. Construction debris and drainage damage soil. Tourist influx then brings more vehicles and further disturbance.

(b) Grazing domestic animals: Cattle and goats eat tender shoots, preventing natural regeneration of forest plants. Their hooves compact the soil, reducing water infiltration. They also spread diseases to wild herbivores and compete for the same food — starving native species.

(c) Tourist litter (plastic bottles, wrappers): Plastic takes centuries to decompose and pollutes the soil and water. Wild animals mistake plastic for food and die from intestinal blockage. Chemicals from plastics leach into the ecosystem. The visual damage also undermines the forest's ecological character.
