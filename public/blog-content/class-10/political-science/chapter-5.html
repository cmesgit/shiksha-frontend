01
Definition & Functions
What parties are and what they do
Official Definition · Political Science
A political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in government — agreeing on some policies and programmes for society with the view of promoting the collective good.
👑
Leaders
Top figures who set direction, contest for top offices and represent the party publicly.
⚙️
Active Members
The organisational backbone — they mobilise voters, run campaigns and implement party decisions.
🗳️
Followers
Supporters who identify with the party's ideology and vote for its candidates in elections.

Six Functions of Political Parties

What Political Parties Actually Do
6 Core Functions
1
Contest Elections
Parties nominate candidates, run campaigns and seek a mandate from voters. Elections are the primary arena where parties compete for the right to govern.
2
Form Policies &amp; Reduce Complexity
Parties reduce a vast multitude of citizen opinions into a few basic policy positions — making democratic choice manageable for voters.
3
Make Laws
Laws are debated and passed in the legislature by members of political parties. Party discipline largely determines how votes go in Parliament.
4
Form and Run Governments
Parties recruit, train and prepare leaders to run governments — as ministers, chief ministers and prime ministers — when they win elections.
5
Shape Public Opinion
Opinions in society are often formed along party lines. Parties run campaigns, issue statements and use media to shape how citizens think about issues.
6
Connect Citizens to Government
Parties provide ordinary people access to government machinery and welfare schemes — acting as a bridge between the state and citizens.
02
Types of Party Systems
One · Two · Multi

Three Types of Party Systems

Party systems evolve over long periods depending on the nature of society, social and regional divisions, history of politics and electoral systems. No single system is universal — each has its own advantages and trade-offs.

Least Democratic
One-Party System
▸Only one party controls the government — no real competition exists.
▸This is not a democratic option — citizens have no real electoral choice.
▸Example: Communist Party of China.
✕ Not Democratic
Stable but Limited
Two-Party System
▸Power alternates between two main parties. Only they can realistically win a majority.
▸Other parties may exist and contest but rarely win enough seats to govern.
▸Examples: USA (Democrat/Republican), UK (Labour/Conservative).
~ Stable but Fewer Choices
India's System
Multi-Party System
▸Several parties compete; more than two have a real chance of coming to power alone or in coalition.
▸Government is formed by coalition of multiple parties — allows variety of interests to be represented.
▸Can lead to political instability. Example: India (NDA, UPA, Left Front).
✓ Diverse but Can Be Unstable

Challenges to Political Parties
4 Critical Problems

Four Challenges Facing Parties

Despite being essential to democracy, political parties face serious internal and external problems that undermine their ability to truly represent citizens.

CRITICAL
Challenges to Political Parties in India
🚫
Challenge 01
Lack of Internal Democracy
Power concentrates at the top with one or few leaders. Parties don't maintain membership registers, don't hold internal elections, and don't keep members informed. Personal loyalty to the leader beats loyalty to party principles.
👨‍👩‍👧
Challenge 02
Dynastic Succession
Top positions are controlled by members of one family. People without adequate experience or popular support get power through family connections. Bad for democracy — merit is replaced by lineage.
💰
Challenge 03
Money &amp; Muscle Power
Parties nominate candidates who can raise lots of money. Rich people and companies that fund parties gain policy influence. Some parties even support criminals who can win elections.
🔁
Challenge 04
Decline of Meaningful Choice
Ideological differences among parties have lessened globally. The same leaders keep shifting parties. Voters cannot elect genuinely different leaders — the same people rotate through different party banners.
05
Reforms
Done & Proposed

Reforming Political Parties

Reformation of Political Parties — Completed & Proposed
Constitutional & Legal Measures
✅ Reforms Already Made
Anti-defection law — Constitution amended so that any MLA/MP who changes parties loses their seat in the legislature. MPs must accept party leader's decision.
Affidavit requirement — every candidate who contests elections must file details of their property and criminal cases pending against them.
Supreme Court orders to reduce the influence of money and criminals in electoral politics.
Election Commission orders — parties must hold organisational elections and file their income tax returns regularly.
💡 Suggested Reforms
Laws to regulate internal party affairs — ensuring internal democracy, regular elections and transparent functioning within all parties.
Parties must give at least one-third of total tickets to women candidates in all elections — a mandatory gender quota.
Decision-making bodies of parties should have a reserved quota for women — not just election tickets but actual leadership roles.
Government funding of parties — state funding of election expenses would reduce parties' dependence on private money and corporate influence.
"Political parties are not just an option in democracy — they are essential. The question is not whether to have them, but how to make them more democratic, more representative, and more accountable to the citizens they serve."

Exam Preparation · Frequently Asked Questions
7 questions · Complete coverage

All You Need to Know

Q1. What is a political party? What are its components and functions?
A political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in government. They agree on policies and programmes for society with the view of promoting the collective good, and seek to implement these by winning popular support through elections.

Three components: Leaders · Active Members · Followers

Six functions:
1. Contest elections — parties nominate candidates to contest elections
2. Policy formation — parties have different programmes and voters choose among them; they reduce multitudes of opinions into a few basic positions
3. Law-making — parties debate and pass laws in the legislature
4. Form and run governments — parties recruit, train leaders and make them ministers
5. Shape public opinion — opinions in society are often formed along party lines
6. Link citizens to government — parties provide access to government machinery and welfare schemes
Q2. Why are political parties necessary? What would happen without them?
Political parties are directly linked to the emergence of representative democracy. Without them, large-scale democracies would be impossible to function. Specific reasons:

1. In a large country, millions of voters cannot directly represent themselves — parties aggregate and represent diverse views
2. Parties gather different views on various issues and present them to the government in an organised form
3. They bring representatives together so that a responsible government can be formed
4. They support or restrain the government in policy formulation — acting both as ruling party and opposition

Without political parties, each legislator would be independent and it would be impossible to form stable governments or hold anyone accountable.
Q3. What are the three types of party systems? Compare them.
One-Party System: Only one party is allowed to govern. Example: Communist Party of China. This is NOT a democratic option — it denies citizens any real choice.

Two-Party System: Power alternates between two main parties. Only they have a real chance of winning a majority. Other parties may exist but rarely win enough seats to govern. Examples: USA, UK.

Multi-Party System: Several parties compete, and more than two have a realistic chance of gaining power — either alone or in coalition with others. Government is often formed by coalitions. Allows a wider variety of interests to be represented but can lead to political instability. Example: India (NDA, UPA, Left Front).

Party systems evolve over long periods depending on the nature of society, social divisions, history of politics, and electoral system.
Q4. What makes a party a 'national party' in India? How many are there?
A party is recognised as a National Party if it meets either of these criteria:

— Secures at least 6% of total votes in Lok Sabha elections OR in Assembly elections in four or more states, AND wins at least 4 seats in the Lok Sabha

There are six recognised national parties in India: INC, BJP, BSP, CPI-M, CPI, and NCP.

State Party recognition: A party that secures at least 6% of votes in State Assembly elections and wins at least 2 seats is recognised as a State Party.
Q5. What are the four main challenges facing political parties?
Challenge 1 — Lack of Internal Democracy: Power concentrates in one or few leaders at the top. Parties don't maintain membership registers, don't hold regular internal elections, and ordinary members don't get information about what happens inside. Personal loyalty to the leader matters more than loyalty to party principles.

Challenge 2 — Dynastic Succession: Top party positions are controlled by members of one family. People without adequate experience or popular support get power simply due to family connections. This is bad for democracy.

Challenge 3 — Money and Muscle Power: Parties nominate candidates who can raise lots of money. Rich people and companies that fund parties gain influence over party decisions. Some parties even support criminals who can win elections.

Challenge 4 — Decline of Meaningful Choice: Ideological differences among parties have lessened globally. The same leaders keep shifting from one party to another. Voters cannot easily distinguish between parties or elect genuinely different leadership.
Q6. What reforms have been made and what reforms are suggested for political parties?
Reforms Already Made:
1. Anti-defection law — Constitution amended to prevent MLAs/MPs from changing parties. If they do, they lose their seat in the legislature.
2. Affidavit requirement — Every candidate must file an affidavit disclosing their property and any criminal cases pending against them.
3. Supreme Court orders — to reduce influence of money and criminals in elections.
4. Election Commission orders — parties must hold organisational elections and file income tax returns.

Suggested Reforms:
1. Laws should regulate the internal affairs of political parties
2. Parties must give one-third of tickets to women candidates
3. Decision-making bodies of parties should have a quota for women
4. The government should fund parties to support their election expenses (reducing dependence on private money)
Q7. What is the difference between a national party and a state party?
National Party: A party recognised across the country. Must secure 6% of votes in Lok Sabha elections OR Assembly elections in 4 states, AND win 4 Lok Sabha seats. Has units in various states. Examples: INC, BJP, BSP, CPI-M, CPI, NCP.

State Party: Recognised within a specific state. Must secure 6% of votes in State Assembly elections AND win at least 2 seats. These are often all-India parties that happen to have succeeded only in certain states and regions.

Important relationship: National parties are increasingly compelled to form alliances with state parties to form governments at the Centre. This has given regional parties significant influence in national politics since the 1990s coalition era began.
