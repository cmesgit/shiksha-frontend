The Belgian Model
02
Case Study 1
The Belgian Solution

How Belgium Solved
Its Diversity Problem

Belgium's constitution was carefully redesigned to ensure that no community could dominate another. The result is one of the most intricate power-sharing architectures in the world — and it has held together for decades.

The Belgian Constitutional Model · Europe
Five Pillars of Power Sharing
How Belgium engineered peaceful coexistence between two unequal linguistic communities
🇧🇪
🏛️Central Government
French and Dutch-speaking ministers are in equal numbers in the central government. Even though Dutch speakers are a larger proportion of the population, neither group holds numerical dominance in the cabinet.
⚖️Special Laws
Some important laws require the approval of a majority from each linguistic group separately — one community cannot outvote the other on sensitive issues. This is a veto right for both communities.
🗺️Regional Governments
Many powers of the central government have been devolved to the state governments of Flanders (Dutch) and Wallonia (French). These regions govern themselves on most local matters.
🏙️Brussels Government
Brussels, the capital and a mixed city, has a separate government with equal representation for both communities — even though French speakers form 80% of Brussels' population.
👥Community Government
A special community government elected by each language group (not by territory) handles cultural, educational, and language-related matters — giving communities cultural self-governance regardless of where they live.

How Majoritarianism Causes Civil War

Sri Lanka: When
Majority Rules Without Limits

Sri Lanka's government made a series of deliberate choices to favour the Sinhala-Buddhist majority at the direct expense of Tamil minorities. Each step deepened Tamil alienation — until there was no path left except armed conflict.

The Road to Civil War
How step-by-step majoritarian policies pushed a nation from diversity to destruction

Independence
Sri Lanka emerges as an independent nation. Population includes 74% Sinhala and 18% Tamil speakers. Both communities have hopes for a shared future.

Sinhala Only Act
A landmark law declares Sinhala as the only official language, completely disregarding Tamil. Tamil speakers — nearly a quarter of the population — are suddenly excluded from official communication.

Governments introduce preferential treatment for Sinhala applicants in university admissions and government jobs. Tamil youth find doors closing systematically.

A new constitution gives the state the duty to protect and foster Buddhism, further marginalising Tamil Hindus, Muslims, and Christians in the national identity.

Tamil Political Mobilisation
Sri Lankan Tamils launch political parties and struggles demanding official language recognition, regional autonomy, and equal opportunity. All demands are denied by the Sinhala-dominated government.

Several organisations form demanding a separate Tamil Eelam state in northern and eastern Sri Lanka. Tensions boil over into full-scale civil war that would last decades.
MAJOR­ITAR­IAN­ISM
Definition · Key Term
The belief and practice of allowing the majority community to govern as it wishes, disregarding the rights, cultures, languages, and aspirations of minority communities.
In Sri Lanka, majoritarianism meant that the 74% Sinhala majority used its electoral strength to systematically strip Tamils of language rights, job opportunities, and cultural recognition — ultimately causing one of South Asia's longest civil wars and costing tens of thousands of lives. The lesson: democracy requires protecting minorities, not just empowering majorities.

Why Power Sharing
Is Desirable

"Power sharing is not a compromise — it is the very essence of democracy. The question isn't whether to share power, but how creatively to do so."
— Democratic Politics II, NCERT Class 10
🕊️
Reduces Conflict
Power sharing reduces the possibility of conflict between social groups. When all communities have a stake in governance, they have less reason to rebel or turn to violence.
🏗️
Brings Political Stability
Governments that include multiple voices and communities tend to be more stable. Exclusion creates resentment; inclusion creates investment in the system.
🗳️
Soul of Democracy
Power sharing is the very essence of democracy — a system defined by citizen participation. Concentrating power in one group violates this fundamental principle.

Forms of Power Sharing

Four Ways
Power Can Be Shared

4
Modern democracies have developed four distinct types of power-sharing arrangements — each operating at a different level of governance, and each solving a different kind of power concentration problem. Together they form a complete architecture of democratic governance.
Form 1 · Legislature / Executive / Judiciary
Horizontal Division
Power shared among different organs of government at the same level. The legislature makes law, the executive implements it, and the judiciary interprets it — each checking the others. This creates a balance of power and prevents any single organ from becoming all-powerful.
Example: In India, the Supreme Court can declare a law made by Parliament unconstitutional.
Form 2 · Centre / State / Local
Vertical Division (Federalism)
Power shared among governments at different levels — national, state, and local bodies. The central government delegates powers downward to state governments, and further to local institutions. This is the basis of federal government.
Example: India's Constitution divides powers between the Union List, State List, and Concurrent List.
Form 3 · Religious & Linguistic Groups
Among Social Groups
Power shared among different social communities. Constitutional and legal provisions ensure that socially weaker sections, religious minorities, linguistic groups, and women are represented in legislatures and administration.
Example: Reservation of seats in Parliament for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
Form 4 · Parties, Pressure Groups & Movements
Among Political Parties
Political parties, pressure groups, and social movements control or influence those in power. When parties contest elections in an alliance and win, they form a coalition government and directly share power.
Example: India's coalition governments of the 1990s where multiple parties shared cabinet positions.
Exam Prep
Frequently Asked Questions

All You Need to Know

Q1. What is power sharing? Why is it important in a democracy?
Power sharing is the distribution of governmental authority among various organs, levels, groups, and institutions so that no single entity holds all power. It is important in a democracy because:

1. It reduces the possibility of conflict between social groups by ensuring all communities have a stake in governance.
2. It brings stability to political order — governments that include multiple voices are less likely to face violent opposition.
3. Power sharing is the very essence of democracy — citizen participation and voice are its defining features.
4. An intelligent division between legislature, executive, and judiciary prevents the abuse of state power.
5. It ensures that decisions reflect broad consensus rather than the will of a single ruler or party.
Q2. Describe the Belgian model of power sharing. Why is it considered unique?
Belgium is a small European country with a complex linguistic divide — 59% Dutch-speaking Flemish, 40% French-speaking Walloons, plus a small German-speaking minority. In Brussels, 80% speak French. The minority French-speaking community was economically stronger, creating tensions.

The Belgian model is considered unique because:

1. The central government has equal numbers of French and Dutch-speaking ministers.
2. Special laws require support from majorities in both linguistic groups — neither can override the other.
3. Powers are devolved to regional state governments (Flanders and Wallonia).
4. Brussels has a separate government with equal representation for both communities.
5. A special community government elected by each language group handles cultural, educational, and language-related issues.

This model has successfully prevented civil strife and is cited globally as a model for managing diversity.
Q3. What happened in Sri Lanka? How is it different from Belgium?
Sri Lanka gained independence in 1948 with a diverse population: 74% Sinhala speakers (mostly Buddhist) and 18% Tamil speakers (mostly Hindu or Muslim), plus 7% Christians.

Instead of accommodating diversity, the Sinhala-majority government pursued majoritarian policies:

1. In 1956, Sinhala was declared the only official language, sidelining Tamil.
2. Preferential policies favoured Sinhala applicants in universities and government jobs.
3. The constitution directed the state to foster and protect Buddhism, alienating non-Buddhists.
4. Tamil demands for equal rights, regional autonomy, and official language recognition were denied.
5. By the 1980s, organisations demanding a separate Tamil state (Tamil Eelam) formed, escalating into civil war.

Unlike Belgium, Sri Lanka chose majoritarianism over power sharing — with devastating consequences.
Q4. What is majoritarianism? How did it affect Sri Lanka?
Majoritarianism is the belief that the majority community in a country should be able to rule however it wishes, ignoring the rights and interests of minority communities.

In Sri Lanka, the government used its Sinhala majority to systematically exclude Tamil people from language rights, educational opportunities, and government jobs. This created a deep sense of alienation and injustice among Tamil communities.

The consequences were severe: Tamils formed political organisations demanding equal rights, which escalated into demands for a separate state (Tamil Eelam), and ultimately into a brutal civil war that lasted decades, causing massive loss of life and displacement. Sri Lanka is the textbook example of why majority rule without minority protection destroys nations.
Q5. What are the four forms of power sharing? Explain each.
1. Horizontal Division of Power
Power is shared among different organs of government at the same level — Legislature, Executive, and Judiciary. Each checks the others. Example: Judges are appointed by the executive but can strike down executive actions.

2. Vertical Division of Power (Federalism)
Power is shared among governments at different levels — central, state, and local. The central government delegates powers downward. This is called a federal arrangement.

3. Power Among Social Groups
Power is distributed among religious, linguistic, and social groups. Constitutional provisions ensure representation of women and weaker sections in legislatures and administration.

4. Power Among Political Parties and Movements
Political parties, pressure groups, and movements influence those in power. When parties form a coalition to contest elections and win, they form a coalition government and directly share power.
Q6. What is the difference between horizontal and vertical division of power?
Horizontal Division: Power is shared among organs at the same level of government — Legislature makes laws, Executive implements them, Judiciary interprets them and checks both. No organ is supreme; each acts as a check on the other. This prevents concentration of power.

Vertical Division: Power is shared across different levels of government — central/national government, state/provincial governments, and local bodies (municipalities, panchayats). The central government has supreme authority but delegates specific powers to lower levels. This is the basis of federalism.

Think of horizontal as side-by-side sharing and vertical as top-to-bottom sharing of the same national power.
Q7. Why should power be shared? Give prudential and moral reasons.
Prudential (Practical) Reasons:
Power sharing helps avoid conflict. When different groups share power, they have a stake in the system and are less likely to rebel. It brings stability and efficiency to governance. It reduces the risk of tyranny and misuse of power by any single entity.

Moral (Ethical) Reasons:
Power sharing is intrinsically desirable — it is the very spirit of democracy. Citizens have a fundamental right to participate in governance. Concentrating power in one group or individual violates democratic principles. Recognising the legitimate rights of all groups to participate in governance is a moral obligation in a diverse society.
Q8. What is a community government? How does it work in Belgium?
A community government is a government elected by people belonging to a specific language community — regardless of where they live in the country. In Belgium, there are separate community governments for Dutch speakers, French speakers, and German speakers.

These community governments have the authority to deal with cultural, educational, and language-related issues. This means that a French-speaking Belgian living in Brussels can vote for the French community government which then manages the schools, cultural institutions, and language policies affecting that community specifically.

This is a uniquely creative power-sharing solution that gives communities cultural autonomy without geographic separation.
