Definition & Core Concept
What makes a government federal?

What Is Federalism?

Constitutional Definition
Federalism is a system of government in which power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units of the country — with both levels enjoying independent authority over their own domains.

In a federal system, there are at least two levels of government — one for the entire country responsible for national issues, and governments at the state level for everyday concerns. Crucially, neither level can simply abolish or override the other — both are constitutionally protected.

7 Key Features of Federalism

✓
Two or more levels of government exist simultaneously over the same territory and citizens.
✓
Each tier has its own jurisdiction in legislation, taxation and administration — their domains don't simply overlap.
✓
The existence and authority of each tier is constitutionally guaranteed — it cannot be abolished by the other.
✓
Fundamental constitutional provisions can only be changed with mutual consent of both levels of government.
✓
Courts have the power to interpret the constitution and adjudicate disputes between levels of government.
✓
Sources of revenue for each level are clearly specified — ensuring financial autonomy and smooth functioning.
✓
Federalism both safeguards national unity and accommodates regional diversity — it does both simultaneously.
02
Types of Federation
How federations are formed

Coming Together vs
Holding Together

Federations form in two fundamentally different ways — from the bottom up, or from the top down. The difference matters because it shapes how power is distributed between the centre and the states.

Type 1
Coming Together
Federation
Independent, sovereign states voluntarily come together to form a bigger unit. They pool their sovereignty to increase security while retaining their own distinct identities. All constituent states generally have equal powers relative to the federal government.
🌍 Examples: USA · Switzerland · Australia
Type 2
Holding Together
Federation
A large, diverse country decides to divide its power between the national government and constituent units. Different states may have unequal powers — some may be granted special status or additional autonomy.
🇮🇳 Examples: India · Spain · Belgium
🇮🇳
India is a "Holding Together" federation — powers are vested in state governments that function under the overall supervision of the Union government. India's diversity demanded this top-down distribution of power.

Key Insight · Language Policy
Why did English survive as an official language despite the Constitution planning to phase it out by 1965?
Non-Hindi speaking states — especially South Indian states — strongly opposed Hindi becoming the sole official language. They argued it would give an unfair advantage to Hindi-speaking candidates in national exams and government jobs. Their resistance was powerful enough to keep both Hindi and English as official languages of the Union. This is federalism working as intended — the linguistic majority could not simply override the minority's legitimate concerns.
06
Centre-State Relations
From domination to genuine sharing

Centre–State Relations:
Before & After 1990

On paper, India has always been federal. In practice, the centre–state relationship evolved significantly — and the turning point was 1990.

Pre-1990
Central Dominance
The central government frequently dismissed state governments controlled by rival parties using President's Rule — often without genuine constitutional cause. The federal balance was heavily tilted toward the centre.
1990
Coalition Era Begins
No single national party won an outright majority. To form governments, national parties had to ally with regional parties. This fundamentally changed power dynamics.
Post-1990
New Culture of Power Sharing
A new culture of respecting state autonomy emerged. Regional parties gained genuine influence at the national level. The centre became far more cautious about interfering in state matters.
Today
Genuine Federalism
India now has a more balanced federal relationship — though tensions remain. The Supreme Court and constitutional norms increasingly protect states from central overreach.

Decentralisation & Panchayati Raj
Power to the people at the grassroots

Decentralisation:
Power to the Village

Even federalism between centre and states leaves most citizens untouched — they live in villages and towns, far from state capitals. Decentralisation brings government to their doorstep. The 1992 Constitutional Amendment made this mandatory and gave it constitutional force.

📜
1992 Constitutional Amendment: Made local self-government constitutionally mandatory. Regular elections, reservations for SC/ST/OBC and women (1/3 minimum), State Election Commissions, and revenue sharing — all became constitutional obligations.

Exam Preparation · FAQ

All You Need to Know

Q1. What is federalism? What are its key features?
Federalism is a system of government in which power is divided between a central authority and various constituent units (states/provinces). Both levels govern the same citizens but have their own jurisdiction.

Key features: (1) Two or more levels of government. (2) Each tier has its own jurisdiction in legislation, taxation and administration. (3) The existence of each tier is constitutionally guaranteed. (4) Fundamental constitutional provisions can only be changed with mutual consent of both levels. (5) Courts interpret the constitution and adjudicate disputes. (6) Each tier has clearly specified sources of revenue. (7) It promotes unity while accommodating regional diversity.
Q2. What is the difference between 'Coming Together' and 'Holding Together' federations?
Coming Together Federations: Independent sovereign states voluntarily come together to form a larger union. They pool their sovereignty for greater security while retaining their own identity. All constituent states generally have equal powers vis-à-vis the federal government. Examples: USA, Switzerland, Australia.

Holding Together Federations: A large country with diverse regions decides to divide power between the centre and constituent units. Different units may have unequal powers — some units may be granted special powers. India is a holding together federation. Example: India, Spain, Belgium.

The key difference is whether states formed the centre (coming together) or the centre created the states (holding together).
Q3. What are the three lists in the Indian Constitution? Explain each.
Union List: Contains subjects of national importance — defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications, currency. Only the Union (central) government can make laws on these subjects. Currently has 100 subjects.

State List: Contains subjects of state and local importance — police, trade, commerce, agriculture, irrigation. Only state governments can make laws on these subjects. Currently has 61 subjects.

Concurrent List: Contains subjects of common interest to both — education, forests, trade unions, marriage, adoption, succession. Both Union and state governments can make laws. In case of conflict, the Union law prevails. Has 52 subjects.

Residuary subjects — those not in any list — are handled by the Union government alone.
Q4. How does the Indian judiciary play a role in federalism?
The judiciary plays a crucial adjudicatory role in Indian federalism. Since both the Union and states sometimes claim authority over the same subject, disputes arise. The High Courts and Supreme Court have the right to resolve such disputes.

The Supreme Court acts as the final interpreter of the Constitution and can declare any law — whether made by Parliament or a state legislature — unconstitutional if it violates the division of powers. This judicial oversight ensures that neither the centre nor the states can overstep their constitutional boundaries, maintaining the federal balance.
Q5. What are linguistic states? Why were they created?
In 1947 and the years following independence, India reorganised its states primarily on a linguistic basis — grouping people who spoke the same language into the same state. This was done to:

1. Recognise linguistic and cultural differences of various parts of the country.
2. Make administration easier — governance in one's own language is more effective.
3. Unite the country by giving linguistic communities their own political identity within the federation rather than letting unaddressed grievances fuel separatism.

Contrary to fears that linguistic states would break up India, the creation of linguistic states actually strengthened national unity.
Q6. What is India's language policy? Why wasn't Hindi made the sole official language?
India's official language is Hindi, but the Constitution recognises 21 other Scheduled Languages. Central government examinations can be taken in any scheduled language, and each state has its own official language.

Originally, the Constitution planned to phase out English as an official language by 1965. However, non-Hindi speaking states (particularly in South India) strongly opposed this — they feared that making Hindi the only official language would disadvantage their citizens in national competitions and jobs.

As a result, both Hindi and English continue as official languages of the Union. This is a classic example of power sharing — the majority language did not simply override minority language rights.
Q7. How did Centre-State relations change after 1990?
Before 1990: The central government frequently misused constitutional powers to dismiss state governments controlled by rival parties and impose President's Rule, even on flimsy grounds. This undermined genuine federalism.

After 1990: India entered the era of coalition governments at the centre. No single national party could win an outright majority, so they needed to form alliances with regional parties. This fundamentally changed the power equation:

1. Regional parties became essential partners in national governance.
2. A new culture of power sharing and respect for state autonomy emerged.
3. The centre became far more careful about interfering in state matters.

This shift made Indian federalism more genuine and balanced in practice.
Q8. What is decentralisation? What changes were made in 1992?
Decentralisation refers to the transfer of power from the central and state governments to local government bodies at the village, block, and district level. It makes government more responsive to local needs and ensures direct citizen participation.

The 1992 Constitutional Amendment was a landmark — it gave constitutional status to local self-government and mandated:

1. Regular elections for local government bodies — mandatory.
2. Reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes.
3. At least one-third of all positions reserved for women.
4. Creation of State Election Commissions in every state to conduct local elections.
5. State governments required to share revenue with local bodies.
Q9. What is Panchayati Raj? Describe its three levels.
Panchayati Raj is India's system of rural local self-government. It has three tiers:

1. Gram Panchayat (Village Level): Covers one village or a group of villages. Consists of ward members (Panch) and a president/Sarpanch directly elected by adult voters. It is the primary decision-making body for village-level matters. Supervised by the Gram Sabha (all adult voters), which must meet 2–3 times a year to approve budget and review performance.

2. Panchayat Samiti/Block/Mandal (Intermediate Level): A group of Gram Panchayats form a Panchayat Samiti. Members are elected by all Panchayat members in that area.

3. Zilla Parishad (District Level): All Panchayat Samitis and Mandals in a district form the Zilla Parishad. Most members are elected; MPs and MLAs of the district are also members. Headed by an elected chairperson.
