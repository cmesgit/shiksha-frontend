Not everything around us is pure. Most substances we encounter daily — air, seawater, milk, alloys — are mixtures. This chapter explores how matter is classified, the types of mixtures, how solutions, suspensions, and colloids differ, how we separate mixtures, and what makes something a pure substance versus a compound.

Understanding purity in chemistry forms the foundation of analytical science — from purifying medicines to separating petroleum products and testing blood in diagnostic labs.

Classification

The Tyndall Effect
When a beam of light passes through a colloid, the colloidal particles scatter the light, making the path of the beam visible. This is called the Tyndall effect. It is seen in milk, fog, and smoke — but not in true solutions, where particles are too small to scatter light. This is also why a beam of sunlight appears visible through a dusty room.
4
Techniques

Methods of Separation

Mixtures can be separated using physical methods that exploit differences in physical properties — boiling point, solubility, density, particle size, or the ability to sublime.

♨️
Evaporation
Used to separate a volatile solvent from a non-volatile solute. When the solution is heated, the solvent evaporates leaving the solute behind. Use: salt from seawater
🌀
Centrifugation
Based on the principle of density differences. The centrifuge spins the mixture at high speed, causing denser particles to settle. Use: cream from milk, blood tests, urine tests
🫙
Separating Funnel
Used to separate immiscible liquids that form two distinct layers. The denser liquid settles at the bottom and is drained. Use: oil and water, slag in iron extraction
🌫️
Sublimation
Used when one component can sublime directly from solid to gas on heating. The vapour is collected and condensed elsewhere. Use: ammonium chloride, camphor, naphthalene, anthracene
🎨
Chromatography
Separates solutes that dissolve in the same solvent based on their different rates of movement on a stationary phase. Use: colours in dye, pigments, drugs from blood
⚗️
Distillation
Separates two miscible liquids with different boiling points by heating then condensing the vapours. Use: acetone + water, purifying water
🛢️
Fractional Distillation
Used when the boiling point difference is less than 25 K. A fractionating column allows gradual separation. Use: petroleum refining, separating air into N₂, O₂, Ar
💎
Crystallisation
Separates pure solids from a solution by forming crystals on slow cooling. Impurities remain in solution. Use: purifying salt, copper sulphate, alum

Types of Change

Physical vs Chemical Change

Changes in matter are of two kinds. Physical changes alter only appearance or state; chemical changes alter the chemical composition to produce entirely new substances.

Physical Change
Change in physical properties only — shape, size, state
No new substance formed
Chemical composition stays the same
Usually reversible (e.g., melting, dissolving)
No energy change required

6
Pure Substances

Elements & Compounds

A pure substance can be classified as either an element or a compound. Elements are the simplest — they cannot be broken down further by chemical reactions. Compounds are formed when two or more elements combine in a fixed chemical proportion.

Elements are classified into four categories:

🔩
Metals
Iron, copper, gold, silver — lustrous, malleable, ductile, conductors
🌑
Non-metals
Oxygen, carbon, sulphur — brittle, insulators, usually dull
🔲
Metalloids
Silicon, germanium — properties of both metals and non-metals
🫧
Noble Gases
Helium, neon, argon — inert, extremely stable, do not react

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is a mixture? What are its key characteristics?
A mixture is a combination of two or more elements or compounds mixed together in any ratio, without forming a new compound.

Key characteristics:
• Composition is variable — no fixed ratio of components.
• No new substance is formed; constituents retain their original properties.
• No fixed melting or boiling point.
• Components can be separated by physical methods (filtration, evaporation, etc.).
• Examples: sea water, air, chocolate milk, alloys.
Q2. What is the difference between homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures?
Homogeneous mixtures have a uniform composition throughout — they exist as a single phase with no visible boundary between components. Examples: sugar solution, vinegar, salt water, alloys.

Heterogeneous mixtures have more than one phase. The components are not uniformly distributed and there is a visible boundary of separation. Examples: sand + sugar, chalk in water, smoke in air.

Each type has three subtypes: solid, liquid, and gaseous. For example, alloys are solid homogeneous mixtures; milk is a liquid heterogeneous mixture (emulsion).
Q3. What is a solution? What are its properties?
A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances.

Components:
• Solvent — the component present in larger amount that dissolves the other (e.g., water in salt water).
• Solute — the component present in smaller amount that gets dissolved (e.g., salt in salt water).

Properties of a solution:
• Homogeneous mixture with uniform composition.
• Particles are extremely small (<1 nm) — not visible to the naked eye.
• Light path not visible (no Tyndall effect).
• Solute cannot be separated by filtration — particles pass through filter paper.
Q4. What are the different ways to express the concentration of a solution?
Concentration tells us how much solute is present in a given amount of solution. Methods:

• Mass % (w/w) = (Mass of component ÷ Total mass of solution) × 100
• Volume % (v/v) = (Volume of component ÷ Total volume of solution) × 100
• Mass by volume % (w/v) = (Mass of solute ÷ Total volume of solution) × 100
• Parts per million (ppm) = (Parts of component ÷ Total parts of all components) × 10⁶
• Mole fraction (x) = Moles of component ÷ Total moles of all components (sum of all mole fractions = 1)
• Molarity (M) = Moles of solute ÷ Volume of solution in litres
• Molality (m) = Moles of solute ÷ Mass of solvent in kg
Q5. What is a suspension? How is it different from a solution?
A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture of a solid and a liquid, where solid particles are suspended in the liquid medium (e.g., sand in water, chalk in water).

Differences from a solution:
• Suspension particles are visible to the naked eye; solution particles are not.
• Suspension particles settle down on standing; solution is stable.
• Light path is visible in suspension; not in solution.
• Suspension can be separated by filtration; solution cannot.
• Suspension is heterogeneous; solution is homogeneous.
Q6. What is a colloid? What is the Tyndall effect?
A colloid is a type of mixture where one substance is dispersed evenly throughout another, but the particle size (1–100 nm) is intermediate — larger than a solution but smaller than a suspension.

Properties:
• Heterogeneous mixture, but particles not visible to the naked eye.
• Light path visible (Tyndall effect).
• Particles do not settle down on standing.
• Cannot be separated by filtration.

Tyndall effect: The scattering of a beam of light by colloidal particles. When a beam of light is passed through a colloid, the path of light becomes visible due to scattering. Example: milk, fog. This is how we see a beam of sunlight through a dusty room.
Q7. How are colloids classified? Give examples.
Colloids are classified based on the states of the dispersed phase and the dispersion medium:

• Solid in liquid (Sol): Mud, milk of magnesia
• Liquid in liquid (Emulsion): Milk, cream
• Gas in liquid (Foam): Shaving cream
• Solid in solid (Solid sol): Coloured gemstone, ruby glass
• Liquid in solid (Gel): Cheese, jelly
• Gas in solid (Foam/solid): Sponge, foam rubber
• Solid in gas (Aerosol): Smoke, automobile exhaust
• Liquid in gas (Aerosol): Fog, cloud

Also classified by interaction: hydrophilic (water-attracting) and hydrophobic (water-repelling) colloids.
Q8. What are the main methods of separation and when are they used?
Each method suits specific types of mixtures:

• Evaporation: Volatile solvent + non-volatile solute (e.g., salt from seawater).
• Centrifugation: Cream from milk; blood cells from plasma in labs.
• Separating funnel: Immiscible liquids like oil and water; slag in iron extraction.
• Sublimation: Substances that sublime directly — ammonium chloride, camphor, naphthalene.
• Chromatography: Solutes that dissolve in the same solvent — separating dyes, pigments, drugs from blood.
• Distillation: Two miscible liquids with different boiling points (acetone + water).
• Fractional distillation: Boiling point difference <25 K (petroleum products; liquid air into nitrogen, oxygen, etc.).
• Crystallisation: Purifying solids from solutions (copper sulphate, salt, alum).
Q9. What is the difference between a physical change and a chemical change?
Physical change: Only the physical properties of a substance change. No new substance is formed, chemical composition stays the same, and most changes are reversible. Example: melting ice, dissolving sugar, cutting paper.

Chemical change: The chemical composition of the substance changes. One or more new substances are formed with different properties. Most chemical changes are irreversible and always accompanied by a change in energy. Example: burning wood, rusting iron, cooking food.

Key test: If new substance forms → chemical change. If only appearance/state changes → physical change.
Q10. What is the difference between a mixture and a compound?
Mixture:
• Components mixed in any ratio; no new compound formed.
• Elements or compounds just mix — no chemical reaction.
• Properties of constituents remain unchanged.
• Can be separated by physical methods (filtration, distillation, etc.).

Compound:
• Elements combine in a fixed definite proportion by a chemical reaction.
• A new substance with entirely new properties is formed.
• Cannot be separated by physical methods — requires chemical methods or electrolysis.

Example: Air is a mixture (N₂ and O₂ in variable amounts); Water (H₂O) is a compound (H and O in fixed 2:1 ratio).
Q11. What are elements and compounds? How are elements classified?
An element is the basic form of matter that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions. Elements are the simplest pure substances. Examples: iron (Fe), oxygen (O), gold (Au), carbon (C).

Elements are classified into four types:
• Metals: Lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductors (iron, copper, gold).
• Non-metals: Brittle, poor conductors, usually gases or dull solids (oxygen, sulphur, carbon).
• Metalloids: Have properties of both metals and non-metals (silicon, germanium).
• Noble gases: Inert, extremely stable, do not react under normal conditions (helium, neon, argon).

A compound is formed when two or more elements combine chemically in a fixed proportion. The properties of a compound are completely different from its constituent elements.
