What is the smallest unit of life? A cell. Every living organism — from a single bacterium to a blue whale — is built from cells. Some organisms are just one cell; others have trillions working in perfect coordination.

This chapter explores what cells are made of, how they're organised, and how each organelle contributes to the cell's survival, growth, and reproduction.

History

Milestones & Cell Theory

Our understanding of cells grew over centuries through discoveries by biologists, microscope inventors, and theorists — each building on the last.

Robert Hooke
Discovered cells (1665) in cork; coined the term "cell"
A. van Leeuwenhoek
Built first simple microscope with biconvex lenses; discovered microorganisms
Robert Brown
Discovered the nucleus inside plant cells (1831)
Purkinje
Coined the term "protoplasm" for living cell substance
Schleiden & Schwann
Proposed the Cell Theory — all organisms are made of cells (1838–39)
Rudolf Virchow
Extended it — all cells arise from pre-existing cells (1855)
Camillo Golgi
First described the Golgi apparatus (1898)

The Cell Theory has three core postulates:

01
All plants and animals are composed of cells — the cell is the structural unit of all living organisms.
02
The cell is the basic unit of life — the smallest entity capable of performing all living functions.
03
All cells arise from pre-existing cells — "Omnis cellula e cellula" (Virchow, 1855).

Transport

Diffusion & Osmosis

Cells continuously exchange materials with their environment. Two passive transport processes govern this movement.

↔️
Diffusion
Spontaneous movement of molecules from a region of high concentration to a region of low concentration. No membrane required. Continues until equilibrium.
Examples: O₂ entering blood from lungs · CO₂ leaving cells · perfume spreading in a room
🌊
Osmosis
Movement of water molecules only from high concentration (dilute) to low concentration (concentrated) through a selectively permeable membrane.
Endosmosis: water enters → turgid cell · Exosmosis: water leaves → plasmolysis

Control Centre

Nucleus — Structure & Chromosomes

The nucleus is the most important organelle — the director of the cell. It stores DNA and controls all cellular reproduction and chemical activities.

⬦ Parts of the Nucleus
Nuclear Membrane
Double-layered membrane enclosing the nucleus. Contains nuclear pores for selective transport.
Nucleoplasm
Fluid matrix inside the nuclear membrane. Contains nucleoli and chromatin.
Nucleolus
Dense body inside the nucleus. Site of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis — produces ribosomes.
Chromatin
DNA + histone proteins + non-histone proteins + RNA. Condenses into chromosomes during cell division.

Chromosomes are classified into 4 types based on centromere position:

⚖️
Metacentric
Centromere at middle — two equal arms, V-shaped.
↙️
Sub-metacentric
Centromere off-centre — unequal arms, L-shaped.
📍
Acrocentric
Centromere near one end — one very short arm.
▬
Telocentric
Centromere at terminal end — rod-shaped, one arm.

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is a cell? What are its key properties?
A cell is the smallest structural and functional unit of life, capable of performing all living functions independently.

Key properties:
• Too small to be seen with the naked eye — requires a microscope.
• Shape and size vary between organisms and within the same organism.
• All living cells perform: respiration, growth, metabolism, and cell division.
• Cells arise only from pre-existing cells (Virchow's principle).
• All cells share a fundamental structure: plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and genetic material.
Q2. What is the Cell Theory? Who proposed it?
The Cell Theory was proposed by Schleiden & Schwann (1838–39) and later extended by Virchow (1855):

1. All plants and animals are composed of cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of life.
3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells ("Omnis cellula e cellula").

The third postulate explained cell reproduction and ruled out spontaneous generation.
Q3. What is the difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells?
Prokaryotic: No membrane-bound nucleus (nucleoid only), no membrane-bound organelles, unicellular (bacteria, algae), smaller and simpler.

Eukaryotic: True nucleus with nuclear membrane, membrane-bound organelles present, usually multicellular. Yeast is a unicellular eukaryote — an important exception.
Q4. What is the cell wall? What are its functions?
The cell wall is the outermost rigid layer in plant cells, fungal cells, and most bacteria. Absent in animal cells.

Composition: Plant = cellulose; Bacteria = peptidoglycan.

Functions: protects organelles, maintains definite cell shape, prevents osmotic bursting in dilute solutions, allows free passage of water and small molecules.
Q5. What is the plasma membrane? What are its functions?
The plasma membrane is the outermost covering of all animal cells, and lies just inside the cell wall in plant cells. It is a fluid mosaic of phospholipids and proteins — selectively permeable.

Functions: selective transport (in/out), endocytosis (engulfs material), exocytosis (expels material), cell communication and signalling, protects intracellular contents.
Q6. What is the difference between diffusion and osmosis?
Diffusion: Spontaneous movement of any molecules from high → low concentration. No membrane needed. Examples: O₂ entering blood, CO₂ leaving cells, perfume spreading.

Osmosis: Movement of water only from dilute (high water concentration) to concentrated solution (low water concentration) through a selectively permeable membrane.

Endosmosis = water enters cell (hypotonic solution) → turgid. Exosmosis = water leaves cell (hypertonic solution) → plasmolysis.
Q7. What is the nucleus? Describe its structure and functions.
The nucleus is the control centre ("director") of the cell. It contains the cell's genetic material and directs all activities.

Structure: Nuclear membrane (double, with pores) → Nucleoplasm (fluid inside) → Nucleolus (makes rRNA/ribosomes) → Chromatin (DNA + histones, condenses into chromosomes during division).

Functions: controls metabolism, stores hereditary DNA, directs protein synthesis via mRNA.
Q8. What are chromosomes? Name and describe the 4 types.
Chromosomes are condensed chromatin threads visible during cell division. Each carries genes (DNA). The centromere is the primary constriction.

Based on centromere position:
1. Metacentric — middle; equal arms; V-shaped.
2. Sub-metacentric — off-centre; unequal arms; L-shaped.
3. Acrocentric — near one end; one very short arm.
4. Telocentric — at terminal end; rod-shaped, one arm.

A satellite = small segment separated from the main chromosome by a secondary constriction.
Q9. What is mitochondria? Why is it called the powerhouse of the cell?
Mitochondria are double membrane-bound organelles in all eukaryotic cells.

Structure: Smooth outer membrane + inner membrane folded into cristae (↑ surface area) + matrix (contains enzymes, its own DNA and ribosomes).

Called the "Powerhouse of the Cell" because it is the site of aerobic cellular respiration, producing ATP — the universal energy currency. Its own DNA supports the endosymbiotic theory of its origin.
Q10. What are plastids? Where are they found and what are their types?
Plastids are large organelles found only in plant cells (absent in animal cells). Have their own DNA and ribosomes.

Types:
1. Chloroplasts (green) — contain chlorophyll; site of photosynthesis.
2. Chromoplasts (coloured) — contain carotene, xanthophyll; give colour to flowers and fruits.
3. Leucoplasts (colourless) — store starch, oils, or proteins.

Functions: photosynthesis, colouring of plant parts, food storage.
Q11. What is the endoplasmic reticulum? Compare SER and RER.
The ER is a network of membranous tubules, vesicles, and cisternae — the cell's internal transport highway.

SER (no ribosomes): synthesises fats & lipids, detoxifies drugs, carbohydrate metabolism.
RER (ribosomes attached): site of protein synthesis & transport, glycosylation; sends proteins to Golgi.
Q12. What is the Golgi apparatus? What are lysosomes?
Golgi apparatus (Camillo Golgi, 1898): stacked flattened cisternae. Called the "post office" of the cell. Functions: packages, modifies, and ships cell products; forms lysosomes.

Lysosomes: membrane-bound sacs with digestive (hydrolytic) enzymes. Called the "suicidal bag" — if ruptured, enzymes digest the cell itself (autolysis). Functions: digests foreign material, removes worn-out organelles, keeps cell clean.
