What is matter truly made of? This chapter answers that question at the most fundamental level — atoms and molecules. From Dalton's atomic theory to the mole concept and gas laws, these ideas form the bedrock of all chemistry.

Understanding how atoms combine, how mass is conserved, and how to count particles using the mole unlocks the ability to calculate, predict, and design every chemical reaction in science and industry.

1
Historical Foundation

Dalton's Atomic Theory

In 1808, John Dalton proposed a systematic atomic theory that explained the laws of chemical combination. It gave science its first rigorous model of the atom.

01
Matter is Atomic
All matter is composed of extremely tiny particles called atoms. Atoms are the fundamental building blocks of all substances.
02
Atoms are Indivisible
Atoms cannot be divided, created, or destroyed by chemical means. They participate in reactions but are not broken down in the process.
03
Fixed Ratios in Compounds
Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds. Different compounds of the same elements have different ratios.
2
Fundamental Laws

Laws of Chemical Combination

Two fundamental laws govern how elements combine to form compounds. These were discovered experimentally before atomic theory could explain them.

Lavoisier · 1789
Law of Conservation of Mass
Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. The total mass of reactants always equals the total mass of products.
Σ mass(reactants) = Σ mass(products)
Proust · 1799
Law of Constant Proportions
A chemical compound always contains the same elements in a fixed proportion by mass, regardless of its source or how it was prepared.
H₂O: H:O = 1:8 always (by mass)
3
Building Block

The Atom — Size, Symbol & Mass

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical identity of that element. It cannot be created or destroyed by chemical means.

📏
Atomic Radius
The size of an atom is given by its atomic radius, expressed in nanometres (nm). Atoms are unimaginably small — a human hair is ~80,000 atoms wide.
🔤
Chemical Symbols
Symbols are 1–2 letters from the English or Latin name of the element. They represent the element and one atom of that element.
⚖️

Measured relative to C-12 isotope (mass = 12 u). Also called relative atomic mass. Expressed in atomic mass units (u).

4
Measurement

Mass Terms — Atomic to Molar

Chemistry uses several mass-related terms. Understanding each precisely is essential for calculations.

Atomic Mass
Mass of one atom of an element. Measured relative to C-12. Expressed in atomic mass units (u). Example: O = 16 u, N = 14 u.
Gram Atomic Mass
Mass of one mole of atoms of an element, in grams. Numerically equal to atomic mass. Example: Gram atomic mass of N = 14 g.
Relative Atomic Mass
Ratio of the mass of one atom of an element to the mass of one atom of hydrogen (taken as unity). Dimensionless.
Gram Molecular Volume
Volume occupied by 1 gram molecule of any dry gas at STP. Experimentally = 22.4 L for all gases.
1 gram molecule of gas = 22.4 L at STP
5
Particles

Molecules & Ions

When atoms combine, they form molecules (neutral) or ions (charged). Both are fundamental particles found in chemical compounds.

Neutral · Covalent
🔗 Molecule
Formed when two or more atoms combine chemically — same or different elements.
The number of atoms in a molecule = its atomicity (mono-, di-, tri-, poly-atomic).
Molecules are neutral — no net electric charge.
Examples: H₂O (2 atoms), O₂ (2 atoms), NH₃ (4 atoms), C₆H₁₂O₆ (24 atoms)
Charged · Ionic
⚡ Ion
Formed when an atom (or group of atoms) gains or loses electrons.
Carries a net electric charge — positive or negative.
Ionic compounds form from the combination of cations + anions.
Examples: NaCl (Na⁺ + Cl⁻), MgO (Mg²⁺ + O²⁻), CaCl₂
+
Cation
Basic Radical
Formed when an atom loses electrons. Carries positive charge. Examples: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, H⁺, Ca²⁺
−
Anion
Acidic Radical
Formed when an atom gains electrons. Carries negative charge. Examples: Cl⁻, O²⁻, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, OH⁻
6
Representation

Chemical Formula & Molecular Mass

A chemical formula uses element symbols and subscript numbers to show what atoms — and how many of each — make up a molecule of a compound.

01
Know the symbols
Identify the chemical symbols of all elements in the compound. Use the periodic table.
02
Know the valencies
Find the combining capacity (valency) of each element. This tells you how many bonds each atom can form.
03
Apply the criss-cross rule
Swap the valencies of the two elements as subscripts. Simplify if possible. Example: Al (valency 3) + O (valency 2) → Al₂O₃
04
Write and verify
The resulting formula should be electrically neutral (for ionic compounds, total positive charge = total negative charge).
Molecular Mass
Sum of atomic masses of all atoms in one molecule. Used for covalent compounds. Example: H₂O = 2(1) + 16 = 18 u
Formula Unit Mass
Sum of atomic masses in one formula unit of an ionic compound. Example: NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u

8
Gaseous Behaviour

Avogadro's & Gay-Lussac's Law

Two important laws describe how gases behave in terms of volume, pressure, temperature, and moles.

Avogadro · Equal Volumes
Avogadro's Law
Under the same conditions of temperature and pressure, equal volumes of all gases contain equal numbers of moles (and molecules).
1 mole of any gas at STP = 22.4 L
Gay-Lussac · P ∝ T
Gay-Lussac's Law
At constant volume, the pressure of a fixed amount of gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature.
P₁ / T₁ = P₂ / T₂

"The mole is chemistry's great bridge — connecting the invisible world of atoms (6 × 10²³ of them!) to the grams we can weigh on a balance. Avogadro's number is perhaps the most important number in all of science."

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is Dalton's Atomic Theory? What are its main postulates?
Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808) laid the foundation of modern chemistry. Key postulates:

• All matter is made of very tiny particles called atoms.
• Atoms are indivisible — they cannot be created, destroyed, or broken further by chemical means.
• Atoms of the same element are identical in mass and properties; atoms of different elements differ.
• Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
• In a chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed.

Note: Later discoveries showed atoms can be divided (into protons, neutrons, electrons), but Dalton's theory remains foundational for understanding chemical reactions.
Q2. What is the Law of Conservation of Mass?
The Law of Conservation of Mass states that mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

This means the total mass of all reactants equals the total mass of all products in any chemical reaction.

Example: If 4g of hydrogen reacts with 32g of oxygen to form water, then 36g of water is produced — no mass is gained or lost.

This law was given by Antoine Lavoisier and is one of the most fundamental laws in chemistry.
Q3. What is the Law of Constant Proportions?
The Law of Constant Proportions (also called the Law of Definite Proportions) states that a chemical compound always contains the same elements in a fixed proportion by mass, regardless of the source or method of preparation.

Example: Water (H₂O) always contains hydrogen and oxygen in the mass ratio 1:8, whether it is obtained from a river, rain, or a laboratory.

This law was given by Joseph Proust in 1799. It confirms that compounds have a definite chemical composition.
Q4. What is an atom? How is it represented?
An atom is the smallest particle of matter that can take part in a chemical reaction. It can neither be created nor destroyed by chemical means.

Key facts:
• The size of an atom is indicated by its atomic radius, expressed in nanometers (nm). Atoms are extremely small — 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m.
• Atoms are represented by chemical symbols — one or two letters derived from the English or Latin name of the element.
• The symbol represents both the element and one atom of that element.

Examples: H (Hydrogen), O (Oxygen), Na (Sodium — from Latin 'Natrium'), Fe (Iron — from Latin 'Ferrum'), Au (Gold — from Latin 'Aurum').
Q5. What is atomic mass? What is gram atomic mass?
Atomic mass is the mass of one atom of an element. It is also called relative atomic mass because it is measured relative to the mass of a carbon-12 (C-12) isotope, which is assigned a mass of exactly 12 atomic mass units (u).

Relative atomic mass = Mass of one atom of element ÷ Mass of one atom of hydrogen

Gram atomic mass (molar mass of atoms) is the mass of one mole of atoms of an element, expressed in grams. It is numerically equal to the atomic mass in u.

Example: Atomic mass of nitrogen = 14 u → Gram atomic mass of nitrogen = 14 g

Gram molecular volume: The volume occupied by 1 gram molecule of any dry gas at STP = 22.4 litres.
Q6. What is a molecule? What is atomicity?
A molecule is formed when two or more atoms of the same or different elements combine chemically. It is the smallest particle of a substance that has the chemical properties of that substance.

Atomicity is the number of atoms that combine to form one molecule of an element or compound.

Types based on atomicity:
• Monoatomic: 1 atom (noble gases — He, Ne, Ar)
• Diatomic: 2 atoms (H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, HCl, CO)
• Triatomic: 3 atoms (O₃, H₂O, CO₂)
• Polyatomic: 4+ atoms (P₄, S₈, CH₄, C₆H₁₂O₆)
Q7. What is an ion? What are cations and anions?
An ion is a charged particle formed when an atom or group of atoms gains or loses electrons, giving it a net electric charge.

• Cation (Positive ion): Formed when an atom loses electrons. Also called the basic radical. Example: Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Al³⁺
• Anion (Negative ion): Formed when an atom gains electrons. Also called the acidic radical. Example: Cl⁻, O²⁻, SO₄²⁻

Ionic compounds are formed by the combination of cations and anions. They are held together by strong electrostatic attraction called ionic bonds. Examples: NaCl (Na⁺ + Cl⁻), MgO (Mg²⁺ + O²⁻).
Q8. What is a chemical formula? How is it written?
A chemical formula is the symbolic representation of the composition of a molecule, showing the symbols and numbers of each type of atom present.

A molecular formula shows exactly which atoms and how many of each are in one molecule.

To write a chemical formula, you need:
1. Symbols of the constituent elements.
2. Valency (combining capacity) of each element — then use the criss-cross method: swap the valencies as subscripts.

Example: Magnesium (valency 2) + Oxygen (valency 2) → MgO
Aluminium (valency 3) + Oxygen (valency 2) → Al₂O₃ (criss-cross: 3 and 2 swap)
Q9. What is molecular mass and formula unit mass?
Molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms present in one molecule of a substance.

Example: Molecular mass of H₂O = 2(1) + 16 = 18 u

Formula unit mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in one formula unit of a substance. This term is used for ionic compounds (which don't exist as discrete molecules but as a lattice of ions).

Example: Formula unit mass of NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u

For molecular compounds, molecular mass and formula unit mass are the same. For ionic compounds, formula unit mass is the correct term.
Q10. What is the Mole Concept? What is Avogadro's number?
The mole is the SI unit for amount of substance. One mole of a substance contains exactly 6.022 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules, or ions).

6.022 × 10²³ is called Avogadro's number (Nₐ).

Key relationships:
• 1 mole of atoms = 6.022 × 10²³ atoms = atomic mass in grams
• 1 mole of molecules = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules = molecular mass in grams
• Molar mass = Mass of substance ÷ Number of moles

The mole concept connects the microscopic world of atoms to measurable macroscopic quantities in the laboratory.
Q11. What are Avogadro's Law and Gay-Lussac's Law?
Avogadro's Law: Under the same conditions of temperature and pressure, equal volumes of all gases contain equal numbers of moles (and hence equal numbers of molecules).

This is why the molar volume of any gas at STP is 22.4 litres.

Gay-Lussac's Law: At constant volume, the pressure of a fixed amount of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature.

Formula: P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂

This explains why a sealed gas container can explode if strongly heated — pressure rises with temperature at constant volume.
