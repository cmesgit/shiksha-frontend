Everything in the universe is made of matter — from the air we breathe to the stars in the sky. Matter is defined as anything that has mass and occupies space. This chapter explores the physical nature of matter, its different states, the transitions between them, and the fascinating phenomenon of evaporation.

Understanding matter at the particle level — how particles are arranged, how they move, and how they interact — unlocks the science behind everyday observations like why ice melts, why sweat cools us, and why pressure cookers cook food faster.

1
Particle Nature

Characteristics of Matter Particles

Matter is not continuous — it is made up of tiny particles (atoms and molecules). These particles have four fundamental properties that explain all physical behaviour of matter:

🔬
Atoms & Molecules
Atoms are the smallest possible units of matter. They combine to form molecules, which are the structural building blocks of all substances.
🌌
Spaces Between Particles
There are inter-particle spaces between matter particles. These spaces are largest in gases and smallest in solids — explaining diffusion and compressibility.
⚡
Continuous Motion
Particles are in constant random motion. This kinetic energy increases with temperature — which is why substances diffuse faster and evaporate quicker when heated.
🧲
Intermolecular Attraction
Particles attract each other. This force is highest in solids > liquids > gases — holding particles together and giving each state its unique properties.

Exotic States

Plasma & Bose-Einstein Condensate

Beyond the familiar three states, matter can exist in two extreme forms under extraordinary conditions of energy or temperature:

4th State · Extreme Heat
🔥 Plasma
Super-energetic and super-excited particles (ionised gas)
No definite shape and volume
Most common state of matter in the universe (stars, lightning)
Influenced by electric and magnetic fields
Neon signs and fluorescent lamps contain plasma
5th State · Near Absolute Zero
🧪 Bose-Einstein Condensate
Super-unenergetic and super-cooled particles
Formed by cooling an extremely low-density gas to near 0 K
Particles overlap and behave as a single quantum entity
Super-fluid (flows without viscosity) and super-conductive
Predicted by Bose & Einstein; confirmed in 1995

Interconversion

Change of State

Matter can change from one state to another when heat energy is added or removed. The heat breaks or reforms intermolecular bonds, changing the arrangement and motion of particles. Temperature remains constant during a change of state as energy goes into the transition, not into raising temperature.

Key principle: During any change of state, temperature stays constant. Heat absorbed (or released) at this stage is the latent heat — used entirely to overcome (or form) intermolecular bonds, not to raise kinetic energy.
Melting Point
Temperature at which a solid melts into a liquid at normal atmospheric pressure. Remains constant until all solid converts to liquid.
Latent Heat
Heat required to change state of 1 kg of material at transition temperature without changing temperature. Stored within the material.
Sublimation
Direct conversion of solid → gas (or gas → solid) without passing through liquid state. Example: Ammonium chloride, camphor.
Dry Ice
Solid CO₂ that directly converts to gaseous CO₂ at room temperature — a classic sublimation example used in cooling and special effects.
5
External Conditions

Effect of Change of Pressure

Pressure, like temperature, can change the state of matter. Applying or reducing pressure shifts the conditions needed for phase transitions, making substances melt or boil at different temperatures.

🔩 When pressure is applied to a substance…
Effect on Melting Point
↓ Decreases
Increased pressure forces particles closer, making it easier for solids to melt. Higher pressure → lower melting point.
↓ Example
Ice under pressure from a skate blade melts, providing lubrication — one reason ice skating works.
Effect on Boiling Point
↑ Increases
More pressure means more energy (higher temperature) needed for liquid particles to escape into gas phase.
↑ Example
A pressure cooker raises the boiling point of water above 100°C, cooking food faster and at higher temperatures.
🧊
Dry Ice (Solid CO₂) directly converts to gaseous carbon dioxide at room temperature and atmospheric pressure — a real-world example of sublimation. It is widely used in refrigeration, fog effects in theatres, and food preservation.
6
Surface Phenomenon

Evaporation & Its Factors

Evaporation is the conversion of a liquid into vapour at any temperature below its boiling point. Unlike boiling (bulk phenomenon), evaporation is a surface phenomenon — only the highest-energy particles at the surface escape into the gas phase.

Four factors control how quickly a liquid evaporates:

📐
Surface Area
↑ Increases rate
A larger surface area exposes more liquid particles to the air. Clothes spread flat dry faster than crumpled ones; a wide plate lets water evaporate quicker than a tall glass.
🌡️
Temperature
↑ Increases rate
Higher temperature gives particles more kinetic energy, so more particles at the surface have enough energy to escape into the vapour phase. Wet clothes dry faster in summer heat.
💦
Humidity
↑ Decreases rate
Higher humidity means the air already contains more water vapour, reducing the concentration gradient that drives evaporation. Clothes take much longer to dry on humid monsoon days.
🌬️
Wind Speed
↑ Increases rate
Higher wind speed continuously removes vapour from the surface, maintaining a large concentration difference between the surface and the air, driving faster evaporation. A fan speeds up drying.
❄️
Evaporation Causes Cooling
Evaporating particles take latent heat from the surface they leave behind, reducing its temperature. This is why we feel cool after sweating — evaporating sweat draws heat from the skin. Earthen pots (matkas) keep water cool because water seeps through pores and evaporates, taking heat with it.

"Evaporation is nature's cooling mechanism — the same principle that drives sweat cooling, earthen pots, and refrigeration. When particles escape as vapour, they carry energy away, leaving the rest cooler."

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is matter? What are its basic characteristics?
Matter is anything that occupies space and has mass. Everything around us — air, water, rocks, living things — is made of matter.

Key characteristics of matter particles:
• Spaces between particles — Matter is not continuous; there are tiny gaps between particles.
• Constant motion — Particles move continuously; the movement increases with rising temperature.
• Mutual attraction — Particles attract each other. This force is strongest in solids, weaker in liquids, and weakest in gases.
• Atoms and molecules — Atoms are the smallest units that combine to form molecules, the building blocks of matter.
Q2. What are the five states of matter?
The five states of matter are:

1. Solid — Definite shape, definite volume, rigid, negligible compressibility.
2. Liquid — No fixed shape but fixed volume, slightly compressible, can flow.
3. Gas — No fixed shape or volume, highly compressible, exerts pressure, rapid particle motion.
4. Plasma — Super-energetic ionised particles, no definite shape or volume; most common state in the universe (e.g., stars, lightning).
5. Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC) — Formed by cooling an extremely low-density gas to near absolute zero; particles overlap and behave as one quantum entity; super-fluid and super-conductive.
Q3. What is the difference between solids, liquids, and gases in terms of intermolecular forces and compressibility?
Intermolecular attraction decreases in the order: Solids > Liquids > Gases.

• Solids — Very strong forces hold particles in fixed positions. Cannot be compressed; rigid with definite shape.
• Liquids — Moderate forces allow particles to move past each other. Slightly compressible; no fixed shape but fixed volume.
• Gases — Very weak forces; particles are far apart and move freely. Highly compressible; no fixed shape or volume.

The compressibility order is: Gases >> Liquids > Solids.
Q4. What is the change of state? What role does heat play?
A change of state occurs when a substance transitions from one physical state to another (e.g., solid → liquid → gas).

Heat energy is the driving force: when heat is absorbed, it breaks the intermolecular forces of attraction, allowing particles to move more freely. The kinetic energy of particles increases.

• Solid → Liquid: Melting (at melting point)
• Liquid → Gas: Vaporisation/Boiling (at boiling point)
• Solid → Gas directly: Sublimation (e.g., ammonium chloride, dry ice)
The reverse processes release heat: condensation, freezing, and deposition.
Q5. What is melting point and latent heat?
Melting point is the temperature at which a solid changes into a liquid at normal atmospheric pressure. At this point, temperature remains constant until all the solid has melted — the absorbed heat is used only to break intermolecular bonds, not to raise temperature.

Latent heat is the heat energy required to change the state of 1 kg of a substance at its transition temperature without any change in temperature. It is "hidden" within the material during a change of state.

There are two types:
• Latent heat of fusion — heat needed to melt 1 kg of solid to liquid.
• Latent heat of vaporisation — heat needed to convert 1 kg of liquid to gas.
Q6. What is sublimation? Give examples.
Sublimation is the direct conversion of a solid into a gas (or gas to solid) without passing through the liquid state.

Examples:
• Ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl) — directly converts from solid to gas on heating.
• Dry Ice (solid CO₂) — directly converts to carbon dioxide gas at room temperature without melting.
• Camphor and naphthalene also sublime.

Sublimation occurs because, at certain pressures, a substance can go directly from the solid phase to the vapour phase without the intermediate liquid phase becoming stable.
Q7. How does pressure affect the melting point and boiling point of a substance?
Pressure has opposite effects on melting point and boiling point:

• Melting point decreases when pressure increases — Higher pressure makes it easier for a solid to become a liquid by reducing the space between particles.
• Boiling point increases when pressure increases — Higher pressure requires more energy (higher temperature) for liquid particles to escape into the gas phase.

This is why water boils at less than 100°C on high mountains (lower atmospheric pressure) and at more than 100°C in a pressure cooker (higher pressure).
Q8. What is evaporation? How is it different from boiling?
Evaporation is the conversion of a liquid into vapour at any temperature below its boiling point. It is a surface phenomenon — only the particles at the surface with enough kinetic energy escape into the gas phase.

Differences from boiling:
• Evaporation occurs at any temperature; boiling occurs only at the boiling point.
• Evaporation is a surface phenomenon; boiling happens throughout the liquid.
• Evaporation is slow and gradual; boiling is rapid and vigorous.
• Evaporation causes cooling; boiling does not produce a cooling effect on the surroundings.
Q9. What are the factors affecting the rate of evaporation?
Four key factors affect evaporation rate:

1. Surface area — Larger surface area → faster evaporation (more particles exposed at the surface).
2. Temperature — Higher temperature → faster evaporation (particles have more kinetic energy).
3. Humidity — Higher humidity → slower evaporation (air is already saturated with water vapour, reducing net evaporation).
4. Wind speed — Higher wind speed → faster evaporation (wind removes vapour from the surface, maintaining a concentration gradient).

This is why wet clothes dry faster on a hot, windy, low-humidity day spread out flat.
Q10. Why does evaporation cause cooling?
Evaporation causes cooling because the process requires latent heat. The particles that evaporate take this heat energy from the surface or the body they are in contact with, thereby lowering the temperature of the remaining liquid and the surface.

Examples:
• We feel cool after sweating — sweat evaporates from our skin, taking body heat with it.
• Earthen pots (matkas) keep water cool — water seeps through the pores of the pot and evaporates, cooling the remaining water inside.
• Applying spirit on the skin feels cool — spirit is volatile and evaporates quickly, absorbing heat from the skin.
