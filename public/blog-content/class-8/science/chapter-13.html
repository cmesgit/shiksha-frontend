Every sound you hear — music, speech, thunder — begins with the same phenomenon: vibration. When objects vibrate, they disturb the medium around them, creating waves that travel to our ears.

This chapter explores how sound is produced and heard, the properties of sound waves — frequency, amplitude, pitch, loudness — and the growing problem of noise pollution.

1
Origin

Sound is Produced by Vibration

🔔
What is Sound?
Sound is produced due to vibrations in a body. When a body vibrates, it produces sound in the form of waves that travel through a medium.
↔️
What is a Vibration?
The back-and-forth motion of an object. One complete vibration = object moves from one extreme → other extreme → back to start. Any part of a body vibrating forces the whole body to vibrate.
〰️ Sound Wave — Anatomy of a Wave
2
Human Voice

Sound Produced by Humans

🗣️
The Larynx — Voice Box
The larynx is the voice box, located at the upper end of the windpipe. It has two vocal cords stretched across it, leaving a narrow slit. When we speak, air from the lungs passes through this slit — the vocal cords vibrate and produce sound. The pitch of the voice depends on the length and tension of the vocal cords. Men have longer vocal cords (deeper, lower pitch). Women and children have shorter vocal cords (higher pitch, shriller voice).
3
Medium Needed

Sound Needs a Medium for Propagation

Sound travels by making particles of a medium vibrate. Sound can travel through all three states of matter:

🪨
Solid
Best conductor of sound. Particles closely packed → sound travels fast. Example: metal rails, walls.
💧
Liquid
Sound travels through liquids. Example: aquatic animals communicate underwater.
💨
Gas (Air)
We normally hear sound through air. Slowest medium — particles are far apart.

🌑 Sound cannot travel through vacuum. Astronauts on the moon cannot communicate without special devices — the moon's surface is a vacuum with no air particles to vibrate. When a body vibrates in a medium, the surrounding particles start vibrating and carry the wave in all directions.

4
Hearing Mechanism

We Hear Sound Through Our Ears

👂
Outer Ear — Funnel Collection
The outer ear is shaped like a funnel. It collects sound vibrations from the environment and channels them inward.
🎵
Ear Canal — Transmission
Sound vibrations travel down the ear canal toward the eardrum — a thin, tightly stretched membrane at the end.
🔊
Eardrum — Vibration
Sound vibrations make the eardrum vibrate. The eardrum then sends these vibrations to the inner ear through tiny bones.
🧠
Inner Ear → Brain — Perception
The inner ear transfers the vibration signals to the brain via the auditory nerve. The brain interprets these signals as sound — and we hear.

Properties of Sound

Amplitude, Frequency, Pitch & Loudness

📏
Amplitude
No unit (displacement)
Maximum displacement from the central rest position. Determines loudness — greater amplitude = louder sound.
🔁
Frequency
f = Oscillations / Time
Unit: Hertz (Hz)
Number of oscillations per second. Determines pitch — higher frequency = higher pitch (shrill).

Exam Preparation

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. How is sound produced? What is a vibration?
Sound is produced due to vibrations in a body. When a body vibrates, it produces sound in the form of waves. A vibration is the back-and-forth movement of an object. One complete vibration occurs when the vibrating object goes from one extreme position to another extreme position and returns back to its original position. Example: When a drum player strikes the membrane, it vibrates. This vibration forces the whole body of the instrument to vibrate, producing the sound we hear.
Q2. How do humans produce sound? What is the role of the larynx?
Humans produce sound using the larynx (voice box). The larynx is located at the upper end of the windpipe. Inside the larynx are two vocal cords which are stretched across the voice box, leaving a narrow slit between them for the passage of air. When we speak, air passes through this slit, making the vocal cords vibrate and produce sound. The pitch of the voice depends on the length and tension of vocal cords — men have longer vocal cords (deeper voice), women and children have shorter ones (higher pitch).
Q3. Why does sound need a medium for propagation? Can it travel through vacuum?
Sound needs a medium (solid, liquid, or gas) for propagation because sound travels by making the particles of the medium vibrate. The particles pass the vibration on to neighbouring particles, carrying the sound wave. In a vacuum, there are no particles to vibrate, so sound cannot travel. Example: Astronauts on the moon cannot communicate without special devices because the moon's surface is essentially a vacuum — there is no air, and the very thin layer of gas has particles too far apart to transmit sound vibrations.
Q4. How do we hear sound through our ears?
The outer ear is shaped like a funnel to collect sound vibrations. These vibrations travel down the ear canal to the eardrum — a thin stretched membrane. Sound vibrations make the eardrum vibrate. The eardrum sends these vibrations to the inner ear. The inner ear converts the vibrations into electrical signals and transfers them to the brain through the auditory nerve. The brain interprets these signals as sound — this is how we hear.
Q5. What is time period, frequency, and amplitude? Give formulas and units.
Time Period (T): The time taken by a vibrating object for one complete oscillation. SI unit: second (s). Frequency (f): The number of oscillations made per second. Formula: f = Number of Oscillations ÷ Total time taken. SI unit: Hertz (Hz). Example: 40 oscillations in 2 seconds → f = 40÷2 = 20 Hz. Amplitude: The maximum displacement of a vibrating object from its central (rest) position. Amplitude determines loudness — greater amplitude = louder sound.
Q6. What is loudness and pitch? How are they related to amplitude and frequency?
Loudness: The volume of sound — how loud or soft it is. Loudness is directly proportional to the square of the amplitude: Loudness = (Amplitude)². Unit: Decibel (dB). Greater amplitude = louder sound. Pitch: The characteristic of sound that helps distinguish sounds of the same loudness. Pitch is directly proportional to frequency. High frequency = high pitch (shrill sound, like a whistle). Low frequency = low pitch (flat/deep sound, like a drum). Example: A whistle has a higher frequency than a drum, so the whistle has a higher pitch.
Q7. What is audible sound, infrasound, and ultrasound?
Audible sound: Sounds with frequency between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz (20 kHz) that the human ear can hear. Infrasound (Infrasonic): Sounds with frequency below 20 Hz. Human ears cannot hear infrasound. Animals like whales, elephants, and rhinoceroses use infrasound to communicate over long distances. Ultrasound (Ultrasonic): Sounds with frequency above 20,000 Hz (20 kHz). Human ears cannot hear ultrasound. Applications: police use high-frequency dog whistles; bats communicate using ultrasound; ultrasound is used in medical diagnosis (sonography).
Q8. What is noise? What is noise pollution and what are its effects?
Noise: Unpleasant, unwanted sounds are called noise. Musical sounds are sounds that are pleasant to hear. Note: A musical sound becomes noise when it is too loud. Sources of noise pollution: Vehicles, industrial machines, loudspeakers/TVs at high volume, home appliances (desert coolers), aircraft engines. Harmful effects on humans: Lack of sleep, irritation, loss of concentration, hypertension (high blood pressure), headache, and impairment of hearing. Control measures: Industries away from residential areas; avoid unnecessary horn blowing; play radio/TV at low volume; plant trees along roads; use silencers in vehicles.
